# Faculty Adoption

**Type:** practice
**One line:** Student AI use is near-universal; faculty sentiment is negative and getting
worse. We are building into a headwind.
**Why we care:** Our project needs an instructor sponsor, instructor-scoped Canvas access,
course materials, and eventually classroom time. Every one of those depends on faculty
goodwill.

## The 2026 numbers

From the Digital Education Council's Global AI in Higher Education Survey (45,000+ responses,
35 countries) and related 2026 reporting:

| Finding | Figure |
|---|---|
| Students using AI | **88%** |
| Faculty using AI | **77%** |
| Faculty who have personally handled AI-related academic integrity issues | **73%** |
| Faculty predicting AI will *decrease* student attention spans | **83%** |
| Faculty citing resistance or unfamiliarity as an adoption hurdle in their department | **82%** |
| Faculty using no AI tools at all | ~25% |
| Faculty not using AI in teaching | ~33% |

**The trend is the alarming part:** faculty intent to use AI in the US and Canada **fell from
76% in 2025 to 67% in 2026** — the lowest future-adoption intent of any region.

A related gap: 64% of faculty report having done AI literacy training, yet fewer than three in
ten students see the results.

Inside Higher Ed's summary of the sentiment: *"Faculty Say AI Is Impactful, but Not In a Good
Way."*

## What this means for us

**1. "AI tutor" is not a neutral phrase to a thermodynamics instructor.** It is adjacent to
the thing that has been generating academic integrity cases in their course. Leading with the
technology is leading with the part they're worried about.

**2. Lead with the integrity story, not the capability story.** A tutor with real guardrails
is a *response* to the cheating problem, not another instance of it. The
[Bastani result](../evidence/bastani-2025-harm.md) is the argument: unguarded AI makes
students **17% worse**; a guardrailed tutor doesn't. Students already have unguarded AI. We're
proposing the guarded alternative.

That framing is honest and it's the one most likely to land.

**3. The assessment question is theirs, and we should raise it first.** If our tutor helps
everyone, homework stops discriminating and the course's assessment model shifts under the
instructor's feet. They will think of this. Raising it ourselves — before they have to —
signals we understand their job.

It's sharper than it looks:
[o3 already outperforms every student who ever sat a real thermodynamics exam](../domain/superstudent-thermodynamics.md).
Take-home assessment in this subject is already broken, with or without us. We can be part of
the conversation about what replaces it.
→ [open question C5](../../docs/03-open-questions.md)

**4. TA buy-in is separate and possibly easier.** A
[Tutor CoPilot for office hours](../systems/tutor-copilot.md) is a much smaller ask than a
student-facing tutor, and it makes TAs' jobs easier rather than threatening anyone's
assessment design.

**5. Don't create instructor work.** The systems that scaled — [Cogniti](../systems/cogniti-sydney.md),
[Maizey](../systems/umich-maizey.md) — let faculty build agents themselves. But faculty who
don't want to build anything are the majority. Our sponsor should be able to help by
*answering questions*, not by authoring content, unless they volunteer.

## Open questions

- [ ] What does our instructor sponsor actually think about AI in their course? **Ask
      directly and early.** Their honest position shapes everything.
- [ ] Have they had integrity cases involving AI? What happened?
- [ ] What would make them comfortable, and what's a hard no?
- [ ] Are other PSU thermodynamics instructors interested, or hostile? A second sponsor is
      insurance against losing the first.
- [ ] What does the PSU AI Center of Excellence know about faculty sentiment here?
