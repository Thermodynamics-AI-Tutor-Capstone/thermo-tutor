# Generating course content with AI — what actually survives review

**Type:** practice
**One line:** Auto-generating practice problems and assessment items works well enough to be worth
doing and **not** well enough to ship unreviewed — **54% of AI-generated MCQs met a basic quality
bar**, with reliability falling exactly at the Bloom levels engineering cares about.
**Why we care:** Our tutor needs a problem bank, hint ladders and misconception distractors. This
is the honest cost of building them with a model instead of by hand.

---

## The rigorous number

**Tourigny, Acosta Simancas, Onen, Nightingale & MacDonald (2026), University of Calgary**,
*"A Rigorous Evaluation of Agentic Large Language Model Workflows for Multiple-Choice Question
Generation in Advanced Engineering Courses"* (ASEE 2026, read in full 2026-09-01).

**Over 1,500 questions** generated by a **Gemini-2.5-Flash agentic pipeline** — preprocess course
materials → generate topics → create MCQs → iteratively refine through structured feedback loops —
from lecture transcripts of a third-year FPGA design course, across all six Bloom levels.

Evaluated on four dimensions: **item writing flaws** (19-factor rubric adapted from nursing
education), **diversity** (Distinct-3), **content alignment** (semantic similarity to transcripts),
and **Bloom alignment** (a separate LLM classifier at **77.4% test accuracy**).

> ⚠ **"Overall, 54% of generated questions met the pedagogical standard of fewer than two IWFs,
> with strong performance at lower Bloom levels but diminishing reliability for higher cognitive
> tasks."**

**Read both halves.** Roughly half of generated items are usable, which makes generation a genuine
labour saver over writing from scratch — *and* **every item still needs human review**, because you
cannot tell which half you have without looking. Budget for review, not just generation.

**And the failure is concentrated where we need it most.** Lower Bloom levels — remembering,
understanding — generate reliably. **Analyzing and evaluating do not**, and those are the levels
where thermodynamics actually lives: choosing a control volume, deciding which assumptions hold,
recognising when a process cannot be reversible.
→ [Bloom's taxonomy](../concepts/blooms-two-sigma.md)

## Three process findings worth copying

| Finding | What to do |
|---|---|
| **"Most gains achieved in the first two refinement passes"** | Iterate **twice**, then stop. Further passes buy little and cost tokens. |
| **Topic-guided generation helps small batches but "degrades performance when too many topics are processed concurrently, likely due to attention diffusion"** | **Generate in small topic-scoped batches.** Do not hand the model your whole syllabus. |
| **"Preprocessing transcripts had negligible impact, suggesting modern LLMs are robust to minor transcription noise"** | **Skip transcript cleanup.** Feed raw lecture transcripts and spend the effort elsewhere. |

The batch-size finding echoes [Ethel's grading result](../systems/ethel-eth.md) almost exactly:
*"using a fine-grained rubric for entire problems often resulted in bookkeeping mistakes… dividing
problems into several parts and using a comprehensive sample solution proved more reliable."*
**Two independent engineering-education teams found that decomposing the request beats enlarging
the context.** That is a general operating rule for us, not a quirk of either task.

## A verification checklist, from the one paper that specifies one

*"A Methodological Framework for Modernizing Engineering Course Content with Large Language
Models"* (ASEE 2026, read in full 2026-09-01) is a phased, deliberately repeatable process:
map legacy text-heavy content to a LaTeX template → relate it to the KEEN **Entrepreneurial
Mindset** framing → **human-in-the-loop instructor review**.

Their verdict is the useful part: *"while LLMs can produce **plausible** course structures, they
are **not yet capable of creating complete and compliant curricula** and must be used subject to
human-in-the-loop oversight."*

**What their review actually checks**, which is a usable checklist for us:

- Correct **notation and equations**
- Proper **computational syntax and behaviour**
- Validity of both **analytical and numerical results**
- **Pedagogical alignment** — objectives met, content fits the timeline, and **definitions and
  foundational material appear before applications**

That last item is [KST's L1 axiom](../systems/aleks.md) restated as an editorial rule: no step
should require something that has not been introduced.

⚠ **Two limitations they name that apply directly to us:**

1. **The reviewer needs dual expertise** — in the subject *and* in evaluating LLM output. *"This
   does make the framework difficult to access for all instructors."* For us that means **review
   cannot be delegated to whoever is free**; it needs someone who knows thermodynamics *and* knows
   how these models fail.
2. **They have no metric for uncertainty or hallucination rate.** Neither does anyone else —
   [the best detectors reach ~0.67–0.73 AUC](../concepts/grounding-and-verification.md). So the
   review is unassisted human reading, and that is the real cost.

## ⚠ The other half of the picture: nobody has validated AI-designed courses

The [UIC/Columbia/Penn State team](../evidence/student-ai-perceptions-2025.md) had three faculty
at three institutions use AI to generate **syllabi, course content, homework, quizzes and case
studies** for Thermodynamics, Material and Energy Balances, and Kinetics. Their reported outcome
is qualitative only — AI *"can enhance course material design by providing instructors with
advanced problem-solving tools and real-time feedback mechanisms,"* while *"challenges such as AI
biases and content accuracy remain significant hurdles."*

**No systematic accuracy evaluation of the generated material is reported.** Treat the
course-design claims as anecdote and the Calgary MCQ numbers as the evidence.

## What this means for our project

1. **Generate the problem bank, review every item.** Assume a ~50% keep rate and plan the
   reviewing hours. This is still much cheaper than authoring from scratch.
2. **Hand-author the high-Bloom items.** The generator is least reliable exactly where
   thermodynamics is hardest. Our
   [misconception catalogue](../../research/domain/skill-graph-draft.md) and the distractors that
   encode it should be written by a person.
3. **Two refinement passes, small topic batches, raw transcripts.** Three free process wins.
4. **A human-reviewed, thermodynamics-specific item bank is itself a contribution.** Nobody has
   published one, and [CycleTalk's authoring pipeline](../systems/cyclepad-cycletalk.md) says to
   validate topic coverage against outcomes before investing in it.

## Open questions

- [ ] Does the ~54% keep rate hold for **worked problems** rather than MCQs? Engineering
      thermodynamics is not a multiple-choice discipline, and nobody has measured generation
      quality for multi-step numerical problems with property lookups.
- [ ] Can a [property-tool layer](../systems/gpthermo-wpi.md) raise the keep rate by making the
      generator's own numbers correct? Plausible and untested.
- [ ] Their 19-factor IWF rubric came from **nursing education**. Is there an engineering
      equivalent, or is that a gap worth filling?
