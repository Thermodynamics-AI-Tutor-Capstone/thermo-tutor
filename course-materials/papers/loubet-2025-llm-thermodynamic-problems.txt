Abstract
Large Language Models (LLMs) have made significant progress in reasoning, demonstrating
their capability to generate human-like responses. This study analyzes the problem-solving capabilities of LLMs in the domain of thermodynamics. A benchmark of 22 thermodynamic problems to evaluate LLMs is presented that contains both simple and advanced problems. Five
different LLMs are assessed: GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and GPT-4o from OpenAI, Llama 3.1 from Meta,
and le Chat from MistralAI. The answers of these LLMs were evaluated by trained human experts, following a methodology akin to the grading of academic exam responses. The scores and
the consistency of the answers are discussed, together with the analytical skills of the LLMs.
Both strengths and weaknesses of the LLMs become evident. They generally yield good results
for the simple problems, but also limitations become clear: The LLMs do not provide consistent
results, they often fail to fully comprehend the context and make wrong assumptions. Given
the complexity and domain-specific nature of the problems, the statistical language modeling
approach of the LLMs struggles with the accurate interpretation and the required reasoning. The
present results highlight the need for more systematic integration of thermodynamic knowledge
with LLMs, for example, by using knowledge-based methods.

1. Introduction
Thermodynamics is an essential part of both science and engineering, and it is key to understanding energy systems and mitigating climate change. It involves some of the most fundamental principles of physics, such as the first and second laws of thermodynamics, and its
applications span almost every field of scientific inquiry. However, while the theoretical foundation of thermodynamics is well-established, applying this knowledge in practice presents a
significant challenge. Solving practical thermodynamic problems often involves not only interpreting complex information but also understanding how to adapt and apply these theoretical

physical principles to specific scenarios. Currently, most of this work has to be done by human
experts, eventually assisted by computational tools, which are, for the most part, designed to
solve domain-specific problems. For example, all process simulation tools used in the chemical
industry contain hard-coded thermodynamic knowledge. Given the importance of thermodynamics, it is not surprising that its knowledge has also been included in ontologies that were
developed for a variety of purposes [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8].
We have recently developed a system for solving thermodynamic problems, called KnowTD,
that is based on such an ontology [9]. This knowledge-based system selects the equations that
describe a given problem from the ontology and then automatically and accurately solves them.
KnowTD currently encompasses only a small part of the thermodynamic knowledge, but it is
extendable and demonstrates the feasibility of knowledge-based approaches for flexibly solving
thermodynamic problems. Another knowledge-based system is WolframAlpha, a computational
engine based on the language Mathematica [10] that uses symbolic computation and real-time
data processing for problem solving and can handle natural language input. It includes some
thermodynamic knowledge but can handle only thermodynamic problems from narrow, welldefined classes. Its strength lies in solving the pertinent equations rather than in finding them.
A different approach to solving thermodynamic problems has become practically feasible
only very recently: Thermodynamic problems can be given as prompts to large language models
(LLMs) in the hope of obtaining a correct answer. This approach is explored in the present work.
LLMs are sophisticated computational models designed to process and generate human-like
text [11]. They operate by converting text into smaller units called tokens, which are numerical
representations of words, subwords, or characters. The models are based on a transformer architecture, which uses self-attention mechanisms to capture relationships and dependencies between
tokens across an input sequence [12]. Through training on large datasets, these models learn to
predict the likelihood of the next token given the context, enabling them to perform various tasks
such as text generation, summarization, and question answering. Their performance is driven by
two primary factors: the size of the training corpus, which influences the diversity of linguistic
patterns they can learn, and the number of model parameters, which determine their capacity to
encode and process complex relationships.
LLMs have advanced significantly in recent years, with several new models launched in
2024, including OpenAI’s GPT-4o [13], Meta’s Llama 3.1 [14], and Mistral’s le Chat [15]. Their
applications, once centered on language tasks, have rapidly broadened. This includes the domain
of problem solving in science and engineering. In the recent past, a number of studies have been
published in which LLMs were used in this domain. These studies often have an educational
background [16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21, 22, 23, 24], and many of them come from the field of mathematics [17, 25, 26, 27, 28, 29]. A mixed picture emerges from these studies: While in certain
applications impressive strengths of the LLMs in problem solving were found, major drawbacks
have also become clear: LLMs are by nature statistical models and so are their answers, i.e.,
prompting the same question generally leads to different answers that are often of significantly
differing quality. Furthermore, prompt design greatly affects the quality of the LLMs’ results,
which often sound convincing but may be hallucinations [25]. Thus, verifying LLM outputs is
essential [17]. This need for scrutiny ties into a broader and ongoing debate about the analytical
and reasoning skills of LLMs: It is often argued that they lack such skills, e.g., when it comes
to graduate-level problems [26]. However, there are also more differentiated views, such as that
of Venkatasubramanian [30], who argues that LLMs have what he describes as “geometrical”
reasoning skills but lack analytical reasoning capabilities. Recently, Yao et al. [31] have shown
2

that step-by-step solutions, known as a ”tree-of-thought”, can be used to improve the problemsolving capacity of LLMs. A concern with LLMs is their opacity. Some of the most powerful
LLMs are essentially black-box models, i.e., the publicly available information on their training
and mechanisms is limited [32]. Hence, empirical testing is the only practical approach to exploring the abilities of LLMs for problem solving. This is what we have done in this study for
thermodynamics.
Previous studies have used LLMs for problem solving in physics, sometimes also including
problems from thermodynamics: Pursani et al. [33] evaluated GPT-3.5-Legacy, GPT-3.5-Turbo,
and GPT-4 on the 110 problems from the US fundamentals of engineering environmental exam.
K. Wang et al. [20] used GPT-4 for 40 problems from a college-level engineering physics course,
and X. Wang et al. [34] introduce SciBench, a benchmark of 869 problems in mathematics,
chemistry, and physics collected from college-level textbooks, and apply different LLMs for their
solution. Other work in the field loosely related to thermodynamics is described in [16, 35, 36].
Our study complements and extends this in different ways: We focus on a single field, thermodynamics, which is studied in a systematic way. Within this field, we carry out tests with two
problem sets of varying complexity and with different LLMs and compare the results. Furthermore, our problems were designed especially for this study, aiming to ensure clarity, consistency,
and variety. The problems resemble textbook problems and are given entirely in text form. They
are rigorously defined to ensure that each problem has a single, well-determined solution. We
provide full access to the problems, solutions, and scoring system used in the Supporting Information, so that they can be reused in future studies, including, eventually, the training of
LLMs. Without employing fine-tuning, we conducted preliminary tests using single-shot queries
to identify the most effective prompting strategy. This process involved evaluating various general instructions and ways of presenting the problems. The optimal prompting strategy, determined through these tests, was consistently applied for the studies on which we report here. All
prompts utilized in this research are disclosed herein. To assess the variance of the answers,
each problem was given to each studied LLM three times in independent queries. The answers
were then graded by trained domain experts based on a standard solution. Interestingly, problems
from thermodynamics have been used very recently as test cases to study whether LLMs can be
applied to assist in grading exams [21]. In a nutshell, the answer is: No.
The research questions we address in our work are:
1. How good are LLMs at solving thermodynamic problems?
2. How does the performance vary with the level of complexity of the problems?
3. How do different state-of-the-art LLMs perform on the same test sets?
4. What changes occur when going from one to the next version of the LLM?
5. What is the variance in the answers when the same prompt is repeated?
6. What types of errors occur?
The results of our study contribute to the important ongoing discussions of the problem-solving
capabilities of LLMs within the scientific and engineering domains. Furthermore, we compare
the problem-solving performance of LLMs with that of knowledge-based systems, specifically
KnowTD [9]. While this study does not explicitly address educational issues, the outcomes may
also offer insights relevant to that field.
This paper is organized as follows: The studied LLMs and how they were used are described
in Section 2. In Section 3, we present the problems that were classified in two sets, a simple and
an advanced one. The scoring method is described in Section 3.4. The results obtained with the
3

different LLMs on the different test sets are presented and discussed in Section 4 and compared
to those of KnowTD in Section 5. We conclude in Section 6.

The prompts were generated consistently for all LLMs. The idea behind prompting was to
think of the LLM as a student in an exam: A single-shot query approach was employed, where no
feedback, intermediate guidance, or refinement was provided. Additionally, the LLMs were not
fine-tuned prior to the problem-solving tasks. Each prompt was introduced with the following
header:
”You are a student in a thermodynamics class. You are given problems and must
find the solution. The answer should be formatted in the same way as the input to
be readable in a standard text editor; do NOT use LaTeX encoding. Write formulas
like this: p 1*v 1 = R*T 1. Write units like this: J/kg. Use a code generator.”
Since le Chat and Llama 3.1 had the tendency to give long and verbose answers, the prompt was
modified for these LLMs by adding:
4

”Please answer in a concise, scientific form.”
Since the responses of LLMs to a given prompt are stochastic, i.e., the same prompt can yield
different answers when submitted multiple times, each problem was presented three times to each
of the studied LLMs. These repetitions were not part of the same dialogue and can, therefore, be
considered to be independent; the LLMs did not retain information or learn from one repetition
to the next. This approach represents a balance between the desire to increase the number of
trials to enhance statistical reliability and the practical constraints of manually evaluating and
correcting each response.
We have accessed the different models via APIs, except for Llama 3.1, which was downloaded and run locally on a GPU. Therefore, we were able to work with the largest available
models at the time of the study, with the exception of Llama 3.1: While the largest model has
405 · 109 parameters, our work was conducted with the 70 · 109 parameter-model.
Several parameters control how LLMs generate their responses. In the following, we introduce the key parameters and report the values used for each LLM, which are the default values
in all cases. The temperature parameter regulates the creativity and variability of the model’s
outputs. Lower temperature values (i.e., below 1.0) lead to more deterministic and repetitive responses, while higher values (i.e., above 1.0) encourage greater creativity and randomness. For
the models in this study, the temperature was set to 1.0 for GPT models, 0.8 for Llama 3.1, and
0.7 for le Chat. Another key parameter is the top p value, which influences the diversity and
quality of the model’s responses by limiting the selection of tokens to those within a specified
cumulative probability mass. A higher top p value (e.g., 1.0) allows a broader range of tokens,
increasing diversity, while lower values narrow the selection. For this study, top p was set to 1.0
for both the GPT models and le Chat, and 0.9 for Llama 3.1. The presence penalty parameter
encourages or discourages the introduction of new topics or tokens. Positive values penalize the
introduction of new tokens based on their prior occurrence in the text, while negative values favor
staying on previously discussed topics. The default setting for presence penalty was 0 for all five
LLMs.
3. Benchmark Problems and Evaluation
3.1. Overview
In this study, two sets of thermodynamic benchmark problems were used, which we will
refer to as simple problems and advanced problems, respectively. Both sets include problems
resembling those typically encountered in an introductory university course in engineering thermodynamics. Solving these problems requires the combination of different elements of thermodynamics. For a trained human, the simple problems are relatively straightforward – much easier
than what we would require in an introductory course exam. The advanced problems approach
the exam level but do not reach it.
All problems are reported in electronic form online1 and in the Supporting Information,
which also includes information on their solutions and the maximum scores that could be reached.
The problems were designed to be fully specified by text and have unique solutions. All tasks
require the calculation of numerical values of variables. The set of simple problems used here is
identical to the set of problems that we have recently used as examples to illustrate the application
of KnowTD [9].

3.2. Simple Problems
The 13 problems in this set are problems in which closed systems that contain an ideal gas
undergo a single change of state from one state of equilibrium to another. The considered changes
of state include different types of processes, including:
• Isothermal, isobaric, isochoric, polytropic
• Adiabatic, non-adiabatic
• Reversible, irreversible.
Heat and/or work can be supplied to the system. The problems in the set cover the feasible
combinations of the main features, which makes this set different from many other test sets used
to evaluate LLMs, in which the problems are basically drawn stochastically from a large pool.
If all problems from the present set of simple problems can be solved, we may assume that
any other well-posed problem from the considered class of problems can also be solved. As an
example, we present Problem 1 here:
Problem 1
A gas in a cylinder is compressed reversibly from v 1 = 0.05 mˆ3/kg to v 2 = 0.02 mˆ3/kg.
The initial temperature is T 1 = 298 K. The process is adiabatic. What is the work
supplied per kilogram of gas?
3.3. Advanced Problems
This set contains nine problems that cover a wider range of thermodynamic knowledge than
the first set. It includes processes with closed and open systems, multi-step processes (including
cyclic processes), processes with liquids and gases, and processes with coupled systems. Some of
these problems also include the contribution of kinetic and/or potential energy. In each problem,
several questions are asked, which must typically be solved sequentially. Each of these questions
has a maximum score. As an example, we present Problem 18 here:
Problem 18
In a test rig, two streams of water are taken from two tanks (1 and 2) in which the
water is stored at ambient conditions (T 1 = T 2 = 300 K, p 1 = p 2 = 1 bar). Tank 1
is located at the height of the rig, tank 2 is at a height of z 2 = 30 m above the rig.
The mass flow rates of both streams are equal. The water from tank 1 is isobarically
heated in a heat exchanger, the water leaving the heat exchanger in state 3 is fed
into a first inlet of an adiabatic mixer. The water from the second tank passes a
line leading it to the rig and is then fed into the second inlet of the mixer in state 4.
The water that leaves the mixer in state 5 is then fed into an isentropic pump with
a mechanical power of 260 W. The water leaving the pump in state 6 is accelerated
in an isentropic nozzle. The velocity of the water leaving the nozzle in state 7 is
c 7 = 30 m/s, the pressure is p 7 = 1 bar, and the temperature is T 7 = 330 K. The
cross-section of the outlet of the nozzle is A 7 = 0.2 cmˆ2.
a) Calculate the mass flow rate of the stream leaving the nozzle.
b) Calculate the heat flow in the heat exchanger and the resulting temperature T 3.
6

c) Calculate the pressures p 5 and p 6.
Assumptions:
• The kinetic energy in states 1 to 6 is negligible.
• All connecting lines are adiabatic.
• The process is stationary.
Fluid properties:
• Water is considered to be a constant density fluid with rho = 1000 kg/mˆ3 and
c = 4.2 kJ/(kg K).
• The gravitational acceleration is g = 9.81 m/sˆ2.
3.4. Evaluation
The responses of the LLMs were evaluated by trained experts as if the LLMs had been given
the problems in an academic exam. Since the task was always to calculate numerical values
of certain variables, an option for the evaluation would have been to check whether or not this
value was correct. While this has the advantage of being unambiguous (if numerical issues are
left apart), it is not fair because a simple miscalculation could lead to a loss of all points despite
a completely correct solution path. Furthermore, considering only the numerical correctness
would introduce a bias in favor of LLMs that have the capability to use Python scripts for calculations, which only two out of the five studied LLMs can. Finally, in some cases, even a correct
numerical value can be found for wrong reasons. Hence, we have decided to use the more tedious method to evaluate the correctness of the solution path, where partially correct solutions
also increase the score. As a rule, in our evaluation, 0.5 points were awarded for each correctly
executed step towards the final solution. We acknowledge that the scores obtained this way are
not unambiguous. To reduce the variability, we have set up a master solution for each problem
with suggestions for intermediate scores. However, this cannot entirely solve the issue of ambiguity, as there are usually many ways to obtain a certain solution. Comparing independent
evaluations of the same answer by different experienced reviewers, we estimate the uncertainty
of the scores to be typically +/- 0.5 points. A technical problem with this evaluation is that it can
only be carried out by experienced personnel, that it requires cross-checking of the evaluation by
different reviewers for non-trivial cases, and that it is time-consuming, which limits the scope of
any empirical study like the one we have conducted.
As an example of our evaluation process, we provide in the Supporting Information a graded
response for the two problems discussed in Sections 3.2 and 3.3. These responses are accompanied by colored annotations, highlighting the awarded points (in green) and the identified errors
(in red).

4.1. Performance Evaluation
Figure 1 gives an overview of the scores of the different LLMs for the simple problems.
Figure 2 shows the corresponding results for the advanced problems. For each problem, the
results from the three independent runs are shown, which allows us to assess the variance of the
answers of the different LLMs. As GPT-3.5 consistently failed to generate meaningful responses
for the advanced problems, we have refrained from including its results in this study. In some
cases, it even terminated with an error. This task failure illustrates the limitations of GPT-3.5
when confronted with complex problems.
Overall, the LLMs perform well on the simple problems (see Figure 1). There is a substantial improvement going from GPT-3.5 to GPT-4, but no significant performance differences
between GPT-4 and GPT-4o. The results of Llama 3.1 and le Chat are overall similar, with neither model excelling nor completely failing, but these LLMs generally perform worse than GPT-4
and GPT-4o. The scores also show the variability of the responses of all models: Neither of the
five LLMs consistently produces the same results when solving the same problem independently
multiple times. This becomes particularly evident for problems that were only partially solved.
The LLMs struggle particularly with Problem 12, which involves an adiabatic process. The
text does not specify if the process is reversible or irreversible, so assuming reversibility is incorrect, yet each studied LLM made this assumption in all three repetitions.
The performance of the LLMs on the advanced problems is shown in Figure 2. As expected,
the performance of all LLMs on the advanced problems is significantly worse than on the simple
problems. Correct solutions were obtained only in a few cases and never in all three repetitions.
Overall, GPT-4o generated the best results; on the advanced problems, it also performed better
than GPT-4 (in contrast to what was found for the simple problems). The results of le Chat,
which performed worse than GPT-4o on the simple problems, are almost as good as those from
GPT-4o for the advanced problems. The variance of the scores for repeated questions is lowest
for the GPT models but of similar magnitude for all models.
Table 2 presents the results of the statistical evaluation of the scores achieved by the different
LLMs on both the simple and advanced benchmark tests. For this purpose, the individual scores
on the different problems were normalized with the maximum number of points for each problem
(completely correct solution). The drop in the average scores for all models when transitioning
from the simple to the advanced problems is clearly observed, along with the performance differences between the various LLMs, as discussed earlier. The statistical evaluation also gives
quantitative measures of the considerable variance in the answers of the LLMs. The standard
deviation typically ranges between 20 – 25% of the maximum achievable score, corresponding
to 30 – 50% of the actual scores obtained. This indicates not only that the models fail to produce
consistent results for repeated questions (see Figures 1 and 2), but also that their performance
quality varies significantly across different problems.

problem.

The errors were classified by the experts who corrected the answers. An overview of the
classification of the errors observed in the benchmark tests is presented in Table 3 for the simple
problems and in Table 4 for the advanced problems. The number count in these tables was
determined as follows: For a problem with a maximum score of Nmax points, if an LLM obtained
N points, the difference Nmax − N was divided into increments of 0.5 points. Each increment
is referred to as a penalty, which was then assigned to one of the error classes listed above. In
case of conflicts in the error class assignment, the class that was deemed to be dominant was
chosen, or, when a given error led to more than one penalty, the penalties were split up between
the pertinent classes. There is obviously ambiguity in this process and the numerical results
should not be over-interpreted, but they still give an impression of how often a given type of
error occurred.
Table 3 shows that not only the number of penalties is much higher for GPT-3.5 than for all
other models, but also the distribution of the errors on the classes differs: For GPT-3.5, context
confusions are most important, which play only a minor role for the other LLMs. Comparing
the results for the other models on the simple problems, as shown in Table 3, it is clear that
the GPT models not only have fewer penalties but also exhibit different error patterns. For the
GPT models, the primary source of errors is wrong assumptions, while for Llama 3.1 and le
Chat, numerical errors and wrong equations are most important. The reason for this is that even
though both Llama 3.1 and le Chat generate code as part of their language modeling tasks, they
do not have specialized code generation capabilities like GPT-4 and GPT-4o.

The picture changes remarkably when going from the simple benchmark to the advanced
benchmark: As expected, there is a significant increase in the number of penalties (see Table 4). However, the deterioration in performance is more important for the GPT models than for
Llama 3.1 and le Chat, which leads to a leveling of the quality of the results. This also holds for
the types of errors that occurred. For all LLMs, the most important errors are now context confusions and wrong assumptions. While numerical errors continue to be significant for Llama 3.1
and le Chat, they are only a minor issue for GPT-4 and GPT-4o, which aligns with the findings
from the simple benchmark (see Table 3). However, if we consider a scenario in which all LLMs
were capable of directly producing correct code and applying it, thereby eliminating the influ12

ence of numerical errors, the differences between the LLMs would shrink, both for the simple
and the advanced problems.
The high importance of context confusions that was found for all LLMs for the advanced
problems is somehow similar to what was found for GPT-3.5 for the simple problems. This
weakness of GPT-3.5 was overcome by the more recent versions GPT-4 and GPT-4o, which
performed well on the simple problems. Whether or not this trend will also hold for future
versions of GPT applied to the advanced problems remains to be seen.
We conclude this discussion with a few specific observations: GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Llama 3.1
occasionally rely on fluid properties from undisclosed sources instead of using the provided data,
which carries the risk of inducing avoidable errors. Additionally, both GPT-4 and Llama 3.1 tend
to generate excessive text that essentially paraphrases problem statements without progressing in
the analysis. This verbose output often results in overlooking crucial solution steps, leading to
superficial and sometimes repetitive answers without obtaining the desired solution. In contrast,

approach to their own responses. While they may initially start with incorrect assumptions, they
frequently correct themselves to find the right solution path. The GPT-4o model, in particular,
tends to stop when it reaches a point where it can no longer solve the problem rather than proceeding with incorrect assumptions. As a result, it makes fewer errors compared to the other
models but often lacks the necessary knowledge to fully solve more complex problems.

4.3. Preliminary tests with OpenAI’s o1-preview model
In mid-September 2024, OpenAI released the o1-preview model, which OpenAI claims to
deliver answers comparable to those of PhD students on challenging benchmark tasks in physics,
chemistry, biology, mathematics, and coding, demonstrating substantial improvements over previous models [47, 48]. Unlike the other GPT models, which tend to provide instant answers
without much internal deliberation, OpenAI o1-preview is built to ”think” step-by-step using
a technique known as ”chain of thought reasoning”. Winter et al. [49] report that this new
model achieved a perfect score on a mathematics final exam for high school students. We therefore decided to carry out a preliminary test with the OpenAI o1-preview model on the simple
Problem 12, which none of the other LLMs were able to successfully solve. This test aimed
to assess whether the advanced reasoning capabilities of OpenAI o1-preview could overcome
the challenges that had previously stumped the other models. The prompt was run three times,
yielding the following results: 0.5/4.5, 4.5/4.5 and 3/4.5. While OpenAI o1-preview was the
first model to produce a correct solution for Problem 12, the inconsistency across the attempts
suggests that it cannot yet be relied upon to consistently generate accurate answers. Even the
most advanced LLMs still exhibit limitations in understanding and reasoning – even for simple
thermodynamic problems. To achieve higher reliability in problem solving, a more robust and
trustworthy approach is obviously needed. The most promising way to achieve this is to integrate
explicit knowledge by combining the LLMs with a knowledge-based system.
5. Knowledge Based Systems for Thermodynamic Problem Solving
While LLMs have demonstrated significant potential for solving problems in diverse domains, the quality of their responses varies strongly, and they struggle in domains that require rigorous application of formal knowledge, such as thermodynamics. The integration of
LLMs and knowledge-based systems is a promising way to address these problems by exploiting the strengths of both approaches. LLMs excel at processing and interpreting large amounts
of unstructured data, while knowledge-based systems offer structured, rule-driven approaches
grounded in expert understanding. Combining the flexibility and adaptability of LLMs with the
precision of knowledge-based methods is therefore highly attractive for solving complex scientific and engineering problems, ensuring both scientific rigor and interpretability in the solutions
provided.
Before we discuss ways to intertwine both approaches in the field of thermodynamics, we
first provide a brief overview of KnowTD, a knowledge-based system recently developed for
solving thermodynamic problems, which will serve as our example. KnowTD [9] is a specialized
knowledge representation system designed to address thermodynamic problem-solving. It is
built around an ontology that encodes thermodynamic theories, material properties, and processrelated knowledge. This ontology is paired with a reasoner that utilizes the structured knowledge
to define, set up, and solve user-specified problems. The ontology was meticulously curated by
domain experts to ensure that it contains accurate and validated thermodynamic knowledge. It
organizes thermodynamic theory into concepts, attributes, variables, and equations, along with
their interconnections and dependencies. Additionally, it contains information on how these
elements can be combined to represent given processes.
14

Currently, only simple problems can be solved by KnowTD, however, KnowTD is a modular system that can be expanded to include additional knowledge in the future. The reasoner in
KnowTD first helps the user enter known and desired information, using the ontology to supplement the problem with related facts. It then constructs a knowledge graph containing all relevant
variables and equations applicable to the specified problem and analyzes this graph, tracing a
path from the given inputs to the target variables. This approach not only provides a numerical
solution but also generates a transparent step-by-step solution path — a guarantee for correctness.
A key feature of KnowTD is its ability to generate traceable and explainable solutions, making it valuable for both problem solving and educational purposes. The knowledge represented in
the current version of KnowTD enables solving all problems from the simple test set presented
in Section 3.2. However, in its current form, KnowTD cannot solve the advanced problems
from the second set presented in Section 3.3 – simply because they are outside the scope of the
thermodynamic knowledge that is currently implemented in KnowTD.
The following section briefly discusses key research questions regarding the hybridization of
LLMs and knowledge-based systems, using KnowTD as an example of the latter. There are two
primary approaches to hybridizing these systems: First, using LLMs to enhance the performance
of the knowledge-based system, and second, leveraging the knowledge-based system to improve
the performance of the LLM.
Using LLMs to enhance the performance of KnowTD. The input to KnowTD is a problem posed
in natural language, which must be translated into a machine-readable format by the user through
a guided dialogue with the system. This task primarily requires text comprehension skills and
basic thermodynamic knowledge to interpret the text. LLMs could facilitate this process by acting as an intermediary, converting the problem text into the structured input needed by KnowTD,
with the ontology serving as the target ”language.” The problem text typically refers to realworld items and processes, while the ontology includes the theoretical elements necessary to
construct the corresponding mathematical model. Each of the theoretical elements of the ontology may correspond to a wide range of items in the real world. For example, a closed system
may represent a tank filled with biogas, liquid in a syringe, or gas in the cylinder of a compressor.
LLMs could assist or even replace users in mapping these real-world items to the corresponding
theoretical elements within the ontology.
Building on this idea, LLMs could also be used to translate the output of KnowTD into
natural language. The results in Section 4.2 show that, although LLMs may introduce errors
in their reasoning, they are still capable of providing clear and coherent textual explanations of
their answers. By translating the graph-based solution path generated by KnowTD into natural
language, LLMs could enhance the usability of KnowTD, making it more accessible and intuitive
for users.
LLMs might also play a role in the reasoning process of KnowTD. Based on the ontology,
KnowTD provides a set of valid equations that describe the problem. However, this set generally contains many more equations than needed to solve the problem. It is a difficult problem
to reduce this large, over-defined set of nonlinear equations to a set of equations that enable the
solution of the problem without containing redundant equations — not to mention finding the
set of equations that enables the most efficient solution. The pertinent equations and their associated variables can be represented as a graph. Currently, single-equation traversal is used in the
reasoner of KnowTD to find a way through this graph that connects the given variables and the
target values that are to be calculated [9]. This approach limits the scope of problems that can
currently be solved. The results of the advanced benchmark set show that some of the LLMs are
15

still able to handle this kind of complex calculations. Therefore, LLMs might be able to support
KnowTD in this step of the reasoning process.
Using KnowTD to enhance the performance of LLMs. There are two primary approaches for
using knowledge-based systems to enhance the performance of LLMs: (i) They could be used in
the training or fine-tuning of the LLM, or (ii) they could be used for advanced prompt engineering
and for the verification of answers of a given LLM.
In the first approach (i), the ontology of KnowTD could be used for training or fine-tuning an
LLM. Recent research demonstrates that the vocabulary, relationships, and reasoning strategies
embedded in ontologies can be integrated into LLMs to enhance their performance [50, 51, 52].
However, further investigation is needed to understand how ontology-based fine-tuning can improve the analytical reasoning required for complex problem-solving tasks. Also, knowledgebased problem solvers such as KnowTD could be exploited to generate a vast amount of sample
exercises and verified solutions, which can be provided as training data for the LLMs. However, while such measures may enhance their performance, they do not fundamentally alter the
inherently statistical nature of these models.
Following the second approach (ii), knowledge-based systems could enhance language models by helping to identify, correct, or even prevent the errors discussed in Section 4.2. Errors such
as inconsistent signs or the use of incorrect equations can be detected by cross-referencing the
language model’s output with the validated equations stored in the KnowTD ontology. Verification could be performed at varying levels. At a basic level, the ontology can be used to confirm
whether or not the equations applied by the LLM exist within the knowledge base. At a more advanced level, the ontology can also be queried to check the context of the equations and whether
they are allowed to be used in the scenario at hand. If problems are identified, the LLM could be
prompted to revise its response, to specify where the error occurred and eventually to also specify additional information on the type and context of the error. In prompt engineering, KnowTD
could, for instance, be used to provide background information and applicable equations.
6. Conclusion
In this work, we have investigated the capabilities of current LLMs for solving thermodynamic problems. Two sets of benchmark problems were used, a simple and an advanced one.
The benchmark problems were designed for the present study and are fully disclosed, as well as
detailed information on the assessment of two of the answers of the LLMs, which was carried
out by trained experts in the same way as the answers of students would have been evaluated.
The variability of the answers of the LLMs – evaluated by running each problem three independent times – was found to be considerable. The standard deviation of the scores obtained in the
repetitions was of the order of 1/3 to 1/2 of the mean score, underlining the statistical nature
of the responses of the LLMs. The errors that occurred are of different nature, ranging from
insufficient understanding of the problem over wrong assumptions to numerical problems. Not
only the number of errors but also their nature varied strongly, depending on the type of problem
(simple / advanced) and on the model. As expected, there was a clear deterioration going from
the simple to the advanced problems in all cases. Interestingly, among the LLMs that were applied to the advanced problems (GPT-4, GPT-4o, Llama 3.1, le Chat), the differences between
the scores were reduced compared to the simple problems. Not a single LLM clearly stands out,
as measured by the scores: In all cases, the differences between the mean scores of the models
were smaller than the standard deviation of the repetitions. Some of the observed differences
16

in performance may result from random variability and not necessarily indicate differences in
capability. However, a clear improvement was observed going from GPT-3.5, which was tested
only on the simple problems, to the more recent versions of GPT models. The development of
LLMs is rapid, new versions can perform very differently from older ones.
The LLMs rarely completely failed, even on the advanced problems, and for some of the
simple problems, some of the LLMs consistently gave correct answers, which is remarkable.
These results suggest that thermodynamic knowledge was included in the training data of all
the studied LLMs. However, overall, the results highlight a critical limitation: The lack of
analytical skills significantly restricts the use of LLMs for solving complex problems in science
and engineering, a verification of their results is always necessary. The benchmark problems we
have provided enable tracking future developments of LLMs and also devising and evaluating
advanced prompting strategies. We question whether relying on general-purpose LLMs alone
will be sufficient for reliably solving problems from science and engineering. The option of
providing the LLMs with additional input, e.g., text from teaching materials, and evaluating their
performance on the same benchmark problems is interesting to explore. Another solution could
be to combine the LLMs with knowledge-based systems to take advantage of the strengths of
both approaches. We have recently developed a prototype of such a knowledge-based system
for thermodynamics [9] and discuss options for the hybridization of that system with LLMs. We
view LLMs and knowledge-based systems as complementary partners rather than competitors.
