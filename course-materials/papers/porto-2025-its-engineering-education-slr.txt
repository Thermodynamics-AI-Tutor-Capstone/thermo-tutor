ABSTRACT Artificial Intelligence (AI) technologies are reshaping educational environments, particularly
through Intelligent Tutoring Systems (ITS) that enable personalized instruction and real-time adaptability.
This Systematic Literature Review (SLR) explores the application of AI-powered ITS in engineering
education, a field where learners often struggle with abstract and technically complex content. Guided by
the PRISMA methodology, the review analyzes 46 peer-reviewed studies featuring components such as
Natural Language Processing (NLP), adaptive learning pathways, real-time feedback, and learner progress
tracking. Unlike prior reviews that emphasize general pedagogical frameworks, this work offers three key
contributions: a focused synthesis of AI-based ITS solutions tailored to engineering education; a novel
analysis of underexplored feature combinations, such as the integration of teacher-facing analytics with
student-facing personalization; and the identification of research gaps in dual-user support for both learners
and instructors. These findings provide actionable insights for researchers and developers aiming to design
next-generation ITS platforms that are pedagogically robust, technically scalable, and aligned with the
discipline-specific demands of engineering education.

A. CONCEPTUAL BACKGROUND

This review adopts a concept- and feature-driven analytical
perspective. To support the taxonomy applied in later sections, this section introduces the core AI techniques and ITS
features that underpin the selected studies.
1) Artificial Intelligence Concepts in ITS

The integration of AI in ITS spans a diverse array of technologies and modeling techniques [5]. To structure this review, we

identified and grouped AI-related approaches based on their
role in personalization, student modeling, and instructional
decision-making.
Language and Interaction Technologies (LIT): Natural Language Processing (NLP) [6] is foundational for
enabling conversational interfaces and semantic understanding in ITS platforms. It supports features such as
dialog-based tutoring, question generation, and reading
comprehension. A specific task within NLP, Machine
Reading Comprehension (MRC) [7], allows systems to
derive answers from unstructured text, enriching interactive learning environments.
Large Language Models (LLMs) [8], including prominent architectures such as Generative Pretrained Transformers (GPT) [9], represent a significant advancement
in AI-driven education. These models empower tutoring
systems with context-aware generation of explanations,
personalized feedback, and dynamic instructional content. Generative AI (GAI) [10], often built upon GPT
foundations, enables rich dialog and interactive support
that closely mimics human tutoring.
Moreover, techniques like Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) [11] enhance factual reliability by integrating LLMs with structured or unstructured knowledge
bases, improving the relevance and correctness of generated responses. Complementing these approaches, Information Retrieval (IR) techniques [12] support keywordbased queries and semantic content navigation, contributing to more effective and personalized learner interactions.
• Learner Modeling and Knowledge Representation
(LMKR): Understanding and representing learner
profiles is central to adaptive instruction. Bayesian
Models (BM) [13], including Bayesian Networks
(BN) [14], offer probabilistic frameworks to model student knowledge and uncertainty. Knowledge Graphs
(KG) [15] are also applied to represent semantic relationships among concepts, learning objectives,
and student interactions. Related algorithms, such
as Depth-First Search (DFS) [16], support traversal and reasoning over graph-based knowledge structures. Abstract Syntax Trees (ASTs) [17] represent
the syntactic structure of student input (e.g., formulas or code) in hierarchical form, enabling precise semantic interpretation and logic-based feedback. Neural architectures such as Deep Knowledge Tracing
(DKT) [18], [19] are commonly employed to model
student progress over time, while learning style models
like the Visual–Auditory–Reading/Writing–Kinesthetic
(VARK) model [20] or the Felder–Silverman Learning
Style Model (FSLSM) [21] are occasionally adopted
to tailor content delivery, though these rely more on
educational psychology than AI.
• Learning Algorithms and Adaptation (LAA): Machine Learning (ML) [22] underpins many ITS features

Online Learning algorithms [27] represent an incremental learning paradigm where models are updated

These methods are well-suited to ITS contexts where

selecting the next instructional resource based on realtime student performance and context, while minimizing
regret over time.
Deep Learning (DL) approaches [28], particularly those
leveraging Neural Networks (NN) [29], are widely employed for their capacity to extract complex patterns
from learner data. Within this scope, Self-Organizing
Maps (SOM) [30] support unsupervised clustering of
learner behaviors, while Multi-Layer Feed-forward Networks (MLFF) [31] perform supervised classification.
More advanced architectures, including attention mechanisms and Long-Term Attention Models (LTAM) [32],
are used for sequential modeling and adaptive content
recommendation.
In addition, pattern mining techniques such as Association Rule Mining (ARM) [33] and FP-Growth (FPG) [34] are applied to uncover frequent behavioral patterns, supporting intelligent decision-making for adaptive sequencing and personalized feedback.
• Decision-Making Systems and Personalization Strategies (DMPS): This category encompasses techniques
that support adaptive decision-making within ITS by
selecting or recommending instructional actions based
on evolving learner profiles and contextual information.
Rule-Based Systems (RB) [35] codify instructional logic
through expert-defined rules, enabling interpretable,
goal-oriented tutoring behavior. Finite State Machines
(FSMs), rooted in Automata Theory [36], offer a procedural framework for modeling learning sequences and
transitions between instructional states, especially in
structured dialog or feedback control. Decision Trees
(DT) [37], as interpretable machine learning models,
are also employed for adaptive decision-making by
hierarchically mapping learner inputs—such as performance or confidence—onto instructional actions like

feedback or content selection. Case-Based Reasoning
(CBR) [38], [39] draws upon prior learner cases to
propose analogous solutions, facilitating experiential
adaptation. Multi-Agent Systems (MAS) [40] simulate collaborative or distributed learning environments,
where autonomous agents manage specific instructional
roles such as progress tracking or feedback delivery. Additionally, Multi-Armed Bandits (MAB) algorithms [41]
optimize decision-making under uncertainty by balancing exploration and exploitation, making them wellsuited for adaptive content sequencing and personalized
assessment pathways.
• Pedagogical Foundations for AI Integration (PFAI):
Although not computational in nature, several pedagogical theories underpin the design and adaptation strategies
of ITS systems.
The Zone of Proximal Development (ZPD) [42], derived
from Vygotsky’s theory [43], informs adaptive scaffolding by guiding instruction within the learner’s potential
development range.
Learning style models, such as Visual, Auditory,
Reading/Writing, Kinesthetic (VARK) [20] and the
Felder–Silverman Learning Style Model (FSLSM) [21],
are frequently used to initialize learner profiling and
personalize content presentation, despite ongoing debate
about their scientific robustness.
Additionally, the constructivist learning theory [44] emphasizes active, learner-driven exploration, which has
inspired the design of systems that support self-paced
learning and personalized pathways. Humanistic approaches, rooted in self-determination theory and learner
empowerment, inform ITS systems that prioritize student autonomy, motivation, and engagement.
While these frameworks are not AI techniques themselves, they are crucial in shaping the logic, constraints,
and goals of intelligent algorithms for personalization,
adaptation, and feedback generation.
This taxonomy of AI and adjacent concepts reflects the
multidisciplinary essence of ITS research. It illustrates how
technical methodologies intersect with pedagogical insights
to create adaptive, personalized, and student-centered learning environments.

B. UNIQUENESS OF THIS REVIEW

This review aims to address a unique set of challenges and
research gaps that remain underexplored in the existing literature. While prior reviews have made valuable contributions to
understanding ITSs and broader AI applications in education,
few have comprehensively addressed the combined integration of real-time feedback, personalized question-answering,
and student progress tracking tailored specifically to engineering education. The following subsection elaborates on
how this review differentiates itself from existing work in the
field.
1) Scope and Contribution Compared to Existing Reviews

Several prior reviews, summarized in Table 1, have offered
important perspectives on ITS and AI-enhanced learning environments. However, their scopes tend to be confined to
specific dimensions of educational technology. For example, Feng et al. [73] concentrate on pedagogical strategies

within ITSs applied to Science, Technology, Engineering, and
Mathematics (STEM) disciplines, with particular attention to
scaffolding techniques and student learning outcomes. Similarly, Soofi et al. [69] explore evaluation frameworks used
in ITS research across diverse educational domains. While
these contributions are valuable, they fall short of examining systems that incorporate personalized question-answering
capabilities or that offer dual support for both students and
instructors—especially within the context of engineering education.
Daradoumis et al. [61] identified challenges in the personalized management, delivery, and assessment of Massive
Open Online Courses (MOOC) systems. Huang et al. [67]
and Feng et al. [74] examined trends in Artificial Intelligence
in Education (AIED), focusing on the field’s evolution, hot
topics, and knowledge structures. However, these works lack
technical depth on AI and ML methods used in ITS, omitting
key technologies like NLP, FL, or CBR. They also overlook core pedagogical concepts such as the ZPD and VARK
learning styles, as well as critical ITS features like real-time
feedback, adaptive content selection, and student monitoring.
Recent advances—including LLMs, affective computing, and
GAI—are similarly unaddressed.
Reviews such as Djunaidi et al. [81] and Ivanova et al. [72]
provide assessments of ITS models and intelligent learning
technologies; however, they do not specifically address adaptive or personalized instructional strategies within engineering education. Additionally, while Mthombeni et al. [76]
investigate the role of AI-powered chatbots in engineering
learning environments, their analysis is primarily limited to
the design and pedagogical implications of chatbot systems.
Broader ITS capabilities—such as diagnosing student misconceptions or delivering individualized learning recommendations—remain outside the scope of their study.
2) Methodological Contributions

This review adopts and extends methodological practices seen
in earlier bibliometric and systematic studies. For example,
Vinh et al. [83] apply bibliometric techniques to explore
broad trends in AI applications in education, while Huang et
al. [67] investigate keyword patterns to map the evolution of
educational AI research. Although these studies offer valuable insights into the structure and development of the field,
they do not investigate the detailed integration of specific ITS
functionalities—such as real-time feedback or personalized
question-answering mechanisms.
Likewise, the mapping review by Feng et al. [74] contributes a high-level overview of AI in education, yet lacks
a focused analysis of how ITS features can be effectively
applied within engineering learning environments. By combining both qualitative thematic synthesis and quantitative
review techniques, this study offers a balanced methodological framework that bridges empirical rigor with practical
relevance in the context of engineering education.

3) Addressing Gaps in Related Work

While prior studies such as Bimba et al. [64] and AbuAlsaad et al. [66] examine elements like adaptive feedback and agent-based learning systems, they tend to isolate
these components without exploring their integration into
a cohesive ITS framework. Furthermore, recent reviews by
Bahroun et al. [79] and El Hadbi et al. [85] acknowledge
the transformative potential of GAI and adaptive ITSs but
fall short of examining how these technologies can jointly
support individualized student learning while simultaneously
providing data-driven feedback to instructors.
In response, the present review adopts a comprehensive
lens, analyzing how systems that combine real-time feedback,
personalized Q&A interfaces, and educator support tools can
address the unique instructional needs of engineering students. This integrated perspective aims to guide the development of ITSs that are both pedagogically effective and
practically deployable.
4) Distinctive Features and Contributions

What sets this review apart is its concentrated focus on ITS
features that simultaneously serve both learners and educators
in engineering education. In contrast to Nye’s work [62],
which emphasizes institutional and infrastructural barriers
to ITS adoption, or Qureshi et al. [82], which broadly surveys personalized learning technologies, this study prioritizes
actionable strategies to enhance engagement and learning
efficacy in engineering-specific contexts.
Additionally, by identifying underexplored areas, such as
the absence of Q&A systems capable of leveraging historical
learner data, this review offers new directions for research
and development. Through its integrative analysis, it not only
highlights current technological limitations but also provides
a roadmap for building ITS platforms that are adaptive,
context-aware, and educationally impactful.
III. METHODOLOGY
A. DATABASES SEARCHED

To ensure a robust and comprehensive literature review, three
primary academic databases were selected: Institute of Electrical and Electronics Engineers (IEEE) Xplore, Scopus, and
SpringerLink. These platforms were chosen based on their
extensive collections of peer-reviewed publications in the
domains of artificial intelligence, educational technology, and
engineering education. Their inclusion allowed for wide coverage of journal articles, conference proceedings, and technical papers relevant to AI-driven ITSs.
B. EXCLUSION CRITERIA

The review focused exclusively on research studies that presented validated solutions or technologies with relevance to
ITSs in education. Prior to executing the search strategy,
specific exclusion criteria were established to maintain the
relevance and quality of the selected literature.
First, only peer-reviewed sources—namely, journal articles and conference papers—were included, while non-peer-

reviewed materials such as books, book chapters, and gray
literature were excluded. Second, to maintain consistency and
accuracy in interpretation, only English-language publications were considered. Third, the search was limited to studies
published between 2010 and 2024, a period characterized
by significant advancements in AI, adaptive learning, and
intelligent tutoring technologies.
Furthermore, the review prioritized studies that directly
addressed educational applications, particularly those involving e-learning or digital learning environments. Articles focusing on generic ML use cases, intelligent systems
in non-educational domains, or those primarily involving
Virtual/Augmented Reality (VR/AR) technologies were excluded, as they fell outside the scope of this investigation.
C. SEARCH STRATEGY

To identify relevant studies, a structured set of search queries
was developed using Boolean operators and domain-specific
keywords. The search strings (see Table 2) were designed
to capture literature related to AI-based tutoring systems,
adaptive learning environments, and their applications in engineering education. Each query was tailored to emphasize
one or more core aspects of the review, such as personalization, feedback mechanisms, or the role of natural language
processing in intelligent educational systems.

1) Decision-Making Systems and Personalization Strategies

CBR approaches leverage historical cases to deliver contextrelevant guidance, enabling ITSs to suggest actions or resources based on successful past experiences. Fang and
Guo [95] proposed a classic ITS with rule-based personalization. The tutor operates based on a structured logic of student interactions, while the ITS provides immediate feedback
and instructional redirection. Hernández et al. [63] proposed
an e-learning environment to enhance user training in Information Technology (IT) engineering projects. Sharma et
al. [108] proposed an ITS to enhance the learning experience of students in programming. Hsu and Li [90] proposed
the Competency-Based Guided Learning (CBGL) self-study
platform that generates personalized guided-learning paths
thanks to a new algorithm called Competency-Based GuidedLearning Algorithm (CBGLA). The CBGLA algorithm is
a rule-based and model-driven mechanism that personalizes learning content presentation based on student competency acquisition and past performance. It includes decisionmaking logic that determines when and how learners progress
through materials. Also, Alasmari et al. [129] proposed a
Python Online Coding Tutorial System (OCTS), which is
designed to assist novice programmers in overcoming learning challenges associated with coding. The system primarily
leverages rule-based decision-making and basic learner modeling for adaptive learning pathways in Python programming.
Similar to RB and CBR in guiding flow and personalization, Tccson and Rodrigo [94] used FSMs within Automata
Theory in the ILSA, an ITS where students are modeled based
on mastery vs. performance goals, a psychological trait that
influences how they use the system. Marcus et al. [116]
proposed an adaptive e-learning platform that employs a
custom rule-based system using IF-THEN logic to deliver
adaptive feedback and teacher support. Özcan et al. [117]

proposed UZWEBMAT, an adaptive and intelligent individualized e-learning environment designed to teach mathematics. UZWEBMAT strongly emphasizes tailoring content
and pacing to individual learner needs—principles rooted
in constructivist and human-centered educational paradigms.
Also, Verdú et al. [118] proposed the INTUITEL system, an
ITS designed to provide personalized learning experiences.
INTUITEL uses DTs, based on the performance level and
confidence level of learners, to trigger feedback actions. Finally, Considine et al. [102] proposed an ITS that aims to
provide personalized guidance and feedback to students as
they conduct experiments. The system is strongly rule-based
and constructivist in philosophy.
The architecture of some ITSs is further enriched by MAS,
where autonomous agents collaborate to manage different tutoring functions such as content delivery, learner monitoring,
or adaptive intervention. Lavendelis and Grundspenkis [123]
demonstrate how MAS can simulate instructor-like support or
peer collaboration, by proposing MIPITS, a MAS-based ITS
that aims to provide adaptive learning experiences by generating unique problems tailored to the individual learner’s
knowledge level and learning style. Nafiseh and Gholam
Ali [113] proposed the Personalized ITS (PITS) framework,
which aims to enhance the estimation of learner abilities
and provide personalized recommendations based on learners’ feedback and performance. Also, Green et al. [93] proposed ChiQat-Tutor, an ITS designed to teach foundational
Computer Science concepts, particularly focusing on data
structures. The system is explicitly designed as agent-based,
employing multiple autonomous agents with specific roles
(e.g., teaching agent, student agent, feedback agent). These
agents collaborate to personalize content and provide realtime feedback based on learner interactions.
Related to MAS, MAB is also used for adaptive content
sequencing and personalized assessment pathways. Prihar et
al. [103] proposed the Automatic Personalized Learning Service (APLS) that provides personalized support to students
based on their learning context and needs. Tekin et al. [112]
proposed the eTutor system, which personalizes online learning, based on the MAB framework, by adapting the sequence
and type of teaching materials presented to each student,
considering their contexts and feedback. This online learning
algorithm leverages regret minimization strategies to personalize education.

instruction and adaptive assessment strategies. Troussas et

responses based on the learner’s input and knowledge level.

3) Pedagogical Foundations for AI Integration

The pedagogical grounding provided by the ZPD concept is
also evident across several ITS designs. Inspired by Vygotsky’s learning theory [43], these systems implement scaffolding strategies that allow students to gradually progress
toward independent problem solving. Roldán-Álvarez and
Mesa [119] proposed a DL-based ITS designed to assist
students in programming courses by providing real-time answers to their questions and relevant examples. Damasceno et
al. [43] proposed STUART, an ITS designed to enhance
the scalability of distance education by providing automated
support to students. Moreover, Wang et al. [126] proposed
POEM, a personalized online education scheme that utilizes
RL to adaptively select educational content based on individual learner performance and preferences. Finally, Liu et
al. [127] proposed Poly-X, an ITS designed to enhance online
learning through various integrated modules, that aims to
provide a personalized learning experience by adapting to
individual student needs and facilitating interactive learning.

There is also some literature about using the VARK model
in systems designed to align content delivery with learner
preferences. Overall, the VARK model is used to support
multimodal strategies that enhance engagement and knowledge retention. Panagiotis et al. [101] proposed APLe, an
agent-based ITS designed to enhance personalized learning in
distance education. Also, Díaz et al. [97] proposed an adaptive e-learning platform that tailors educational content for
engineering students studying object-oriented programming
in Java. Finally, Kohlhase et al. [128], in the VoLL-KI project,
proposed a modular toolbox of AI-based educational technology components designed to support personalized learning in
higher education. The system supports exploratory, studentdriven learning paths with continuous adjustment to learner
behavior—aligned with constructivist principles.
4) Language and Interaction Technologies

NLP, on the other hand, is a foundational component in
systems that rely on conversational agents or open-ended
learner input. It enables dialog-based interactions and semantic analysis that underpin real-time feedback and intelligent question-answering mechanisms. Deveci Topal [104]
proposed a chatbot designed to assist 5th -grade students in
learning about "Matter and the Changing State of Matter".
Sakulwichitsintu [124] proposed ParichartBOT, a chatbot
designed to provide automatic answers to questions posed by
master’s degree students. Binh et al. [131] proposed WinITS,
an ITS that combines learning styles, students’ performance,
prerequisite relationships, and real-time responsiveness to
adaptively present educational content. Morgan et al. [110]
proposed ElectronixTutor, an ITS that used NLP to engage
in mixed-initiative dialogue with learners. It supports dialogbased tutoring with semantically rich interactions to enhance
the teaching of electronics. Also, Alshaikh et al. [114] proposed a chatbot that automates the creation of dialogues based
on Java code examples, providing tailored tutoring experiences. AST is employed to parse and understand student
answers in structured formats, especially for mathematical
and logical expressions.
Recent literature also reflects growing interest in the use
of LLMs for open-domain tutoring and educational chatbot
design, by exploring the GAI capabilities of LLMs to enhance
natural interaction and dynamic content generation. Šarčević et al. [111] proposed an open-source GAI Chatbot, based
on RAG, to assist students in the "Introduction to Programming" course. Allen et al. [130] proposed Q-Module-Bot, a
GPT-based question and answer system designed to assist
students with inquiries related to specific module content.

inform instructional decisions, as shown by Taufik and Nurjanah [68] and K-Hetmańska and Nguyen [91]. Particularly,

V. CROSS REFERENCE ANALYSIS

A closer look at the compiled evidence reveals several important trends regarding the interplay between instructional
features and the macro categories of techniques used to implement them. Certain macro categories consistently underpin
specific instructional features, suggesting a level of methodological alignment between pedagogical goals and technological strategies. Table 5 represents a cross-referencing matrix
between ITS features and macro categories, which can be
used as a framework to analyze correlations between ITS
system features and their underlying conceptual foundations
across publications.
Across the corpus, DMPS stands out as the most widely
applied macro category. Its presence is particularly dominant
in features such as RTF, SSG, CA, and CPT, all of which rely
on rule-based logic, instructional sequencing, and automated
pedagogical intervention. For instance, RTF is repeatedly
enabled through DMPS logic in systems like those proposed
by Hsu and Li [90], Green et al. [93], and Marcus et al. [116],

which use pedagogically informed rule engines or behaviortriggered actions to deliver feedback in real time.
However, some features exhibit more nuanced implementation patterns. PLP and ACS are examples where multiple
macro categories are involved, most notably DMPS, LAA,
and PFAI. This suggests that personalization requires both
pedagogical logic and data-driven profiling. For instance, the
PLP component in Bradáč et al. [106] and Wang et al. [126]
is driven by LAA techniques to infer student profiles, while
other studies like Panagiotis et al. [101] use DMPS to guide
progression paths.
LAA is the backbone of SKR and SAE, where statistical
inference, clustering, and feature extraction play a vital role.
In the case of SKR, LAA methods are used extensively across
studies such as Cabada et al. [98], Castro-Schez et al. [100],
and Troussas et al. [125], each leveraging analytics to infer
cognitive states, knowledge levels, or conceptual maps. SAE
implementations follow similar lines, with predictive models
built from usage traces and assessment history.
Meanwhile, PFAI techniques are strongly associated with
features that rely on state estimation or anticipation, such
as AR and ASLL. AR, for example, is primarily enabled
using multimodal emotion detection or sentiment analysis
models, as seen in Cabada et al. [98], Zhang et al. [109], and
Alshaikh et al. [114].
LIT unsurprisingly dominate the implementation of QAS,
where natural language understanding and dialogue generation are essential. Systems like the ones proposed by Deveci Topal et al. [104], Šarčević et al. [111], and Allen et
al. [130] showcase conversational agents that integrate QAS
functionality using LIT-based NLP frameworks. While LIT
appears in other features sporadically, its primary use remains
grounded in dialogic interfaces.
Interestingly, LMKR, though less common overall, appears in features where deep contextualization of the learner
is required, such as PTP and AUTH. In K-Hetmańska and
Nguyen [91], AUTH is driven by knowledge-based profiling,
while PTP again in K-Hetmańska and Nguyen [91] and Liu et
al. [127] reflects LMKR’s ability to formalize instructional
workflows through modeling.
Notably, some ITS features are implemented through multiple macro categories, either within the same work or across
different implementations. RTF, for example, is built using
LAA in Singh et al. [92], DMPS in Green et al. [93], and even
LMKR in K-Hetmańska and Nguyen [91]. This diversity
illustrates that implementation strategies are often shaped by
system architecture, available data, and design intent, not only
by the nature of the instructional feature.
Lastly, there are clear dominant approaches per feature.
DMPS techniques are central to pedagogically grounded
functionalities (RTF, SSG, CA, CPT), while LAA underpins
analytic profiling (SKR, SAE), and PFAI is indispensable for
affective and predictive tasks (AR, ASLL). LIT is reserved
for interaction-heavy systems (QAS), and LMKR appears
selectively where formal representations are essential.

A. RESEARCH GAPS IDENTIFICATION

The review of the literature reveals that most chatbot-based
ITSs focus narrowly on programming-related support. For
example, Šarčević et al. [111] observes that systems like those
by Roldán-Álvarez and Mesa [119] and Alasmari et al. [129]
assist learners in solving coding exercises. Other questionand-answer systems serve a more general pedagogical role.
Alshaikh et al. [114], for instance, developed a system to
foster critical thinking through guided questioning.
However, deeper analysis of the heatmap (Figure 7) shows
that many QAS offering RTF fail to provide personalized
guidance. They deliver generic responses regardless of user
background or past performance. For example, Sakulwichitsintu [124] introduced ParichartBOT, which offers automated answers to graduate students at Sukhothai Thammathirat Open University. While efficient in delivering information, it lacks mechanisms to tailor responses or track user
history for contextual adaptation.

Similarly, Allen et al. [130] developed Q-Module-Bot, a
GAI-based assistant for module-related queries. Although it
enhances accessibility and reduces teacher workload, it does
not personalize content or recommend resources based on
individual student needs.
Only one reviewed system explicitly adapts Q&A responses based on learner profiles. Damasceno et al. [43]
presented STUART, which provides proactive pedagogical
recommendations based on student backgrounds. However,
the system is not tailored specifically for engineering education, leaving a gap in addressing the challenges of abstract,
domain-specific knowledge.
CPT and TASP are similarly underrepresented. Figure 6
confirms the low adoption of these teacher-oriented features.
For instance, Marcus et al. [116] employs solution trace
graphs to help teachers reflect on student progress. Yet, unlike
Özcan et al. [117], this system does not provide learners with
insights into their own misconceptions, a critical component
for self-regulated learning.
Moreover, the heatmap reveals a negative correlation between QAS and features like OSP for both learners and
educators. This implies that these capabilities are rarely combined in existing systems. Addressing this gap could lead to
more comprehensive ITS that serve both instructional and
learning needs.

B. ANSWER TO RQ2: DUAL-USER SUPPORT AND DESIGN
GAPS IN CURRENT ITS IMPLEMENTATIONS

While many ITS solutions are designed to support student
learning, far fewer provide explicit support for instructors,
such as access to analytics or customization tools. Features
that enable dual-user support, such as TASP, CPT, and
ASLL, were among the least implemented, appearing in
fewer than 10 studies each.
Only a few systems, such as STUART [43], were found
to proactively support both learners and educators by combining learner profiling with data-driven recommendations
and instructor-facing dashboards. In contrast, most QAS reviewed, like ParichartBOT [124] and Q-Module-Bot [130],
focused exclusively on student interaction and lacked mechanisms to adapt content or provide feedback based on prior
learner data or performance history.
This imbalance indicates a design gap: while ITS research increasingly prioritizes personalization for learners,
instructor support remains limited and underutilized. The review suggests that more comprehensive dual-user ITSs could
enhance instructional decision-making, improve curriculum
adaptation, and ultimately increase educational impact.
C. SYNTHESIS AND IMPLICATIONS

Together, the answers to RQ1 and RQ2 highlight both the
advances and limitations of current AI-based ITS implementations in engineering education. While personalization and
feedback mechanisms are well-supported by diverse AI techniques, instructor-oriented functionalities and holistic learning analytics remain underdeveloped. Bridging this gap requires the integration of dual-user support into ITS design,
along with context-aware mechanisms capable of leveraging
both learner behavior and pedagogical intent.
These findings point to actionable directions for future research: enhancing the synergy between student-facing adaptivity and teacher-facing insight, developing explainable
models for dual-feedback loops, and advancing scalable ITS
architectures that support domain-specific learning needs in
engineering and beyond.
VII. CONCLUSION

This SLR examined the role of AI in the design and implementation of ITSs within engineering education. By analyzing 46 peer-reviewed studies, the review identified prevalent
AI techniques, such as NLP, ML, and FL, and their integration
into features like adaptive content selection, real-time feedback, and student progress tracking. These features contribute
significantly to the personalization of learning pathways and

the timely diagnosis of learning gaps in technically demanding educational domains.
Furthermore, the study highlighted an underexplored but
crucial area in ITS development: dual-user support for both
students and instructors. While most systems focus on student
adaptivity, relatively few offer instructor-facing tools such as
analytics dashboards or mechanisms for content customization. This imbalance suggests a need for future ITS platforms
to adopt more comprehensive and context-aware designs that
serve both ends of the teaching–learning spectrum.
This review is not without limitations. First, only three
major databases (IEEE Xplore, Scopus, and SpringerLink)
were used, excluding others like ACM Digital Library due
to interoperability constraints. Second, the review focused
exclusively on peer-reviewed journal articles and conference
papers published in English between 2010 and 2024, potentially overlooking relevant non-English, gray literature, and
very recent pre-print studies. Additionally, while the review
provides a broad synthesis of AI-based ITS, it does not include a quantitative meta-analysis of effect sizes or learning
outcomes.
The findings of this review suggest several promising directions for future work:
Instructor-facing Features: Future ITS research should
prioritize the development of teacher-oriented tools, including real-time class analytics, predictive dashboards,
and customizable learning sequences.
• Explainability and Trustworthiness: As ITS increasingly
incorporates complex AI models (e.g., LLMs), there
is a growing need for explainable AI mechanisms that
provide transparency in both feedback and decisionmaking.
• Emotion and Engagement Detection: Affective computing remains rare in current ITS implementations. Future
systems should explore integrating emotion recognition
with adaptive feedback to enhance learner engagement
and motivation.
• Domain-specific and Multimodal ITS: Few ITS are tailored for advanced engineering subfields (e.g., control
systems, thermodynamics). There is also a lack of ITS
that combine multiple interaction modalities (e.g., voice,
diagrams, code) to support diverse learner profiles.
• Scalability and Real-world Deployment: Further research should focus on scalable, cloud-based ITS architectures that can be deployed across institutions, with
real-world validation beyond controlled lab environments.
•

In summary, AI-driven ITS have made significant progress
in supporting individualized learning experiences in engineering education. However, fully realizing their potential
will require interdisciplinary efforts to bridge current design
gaps, enhance dual-user support, and improve the pedagogical robustness of these systems.
