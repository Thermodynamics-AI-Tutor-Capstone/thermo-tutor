Abstract
The advancement of modern thermodynamic technology requires educational software capable of accurately
calculating and visualizing the thermophysical properties of water and steam in accordance with the IAPWS-IF97
Industrial Formulation. This study presents the development of a standalone Python-based Steam Table application
that integrates two thermodynamic property calculation backends, IAPWS-IF97 and CoolProp, into a single
executable (.exe) package that operates without any additional installation. The application performs real-time
calculations of thermodynamic properties, including enthalpy, entropy, pressure, and temperature, and provides
interactive visualizations on h–s (Mollier) and T–s diagrams. The novelty of this research lies in integrating dual
thermodynamic engines into an automated build-release system based on batch scripting, enabling efficient,
reproducible compilation, version control, and distribution. Validation results show that the application operates
stably in Windows 10–11 environments without external dependencies, producing results consistent with the IAPWSIF97 reference tables, with a maximum deviation of less than ±0.05% across all tested state points. This tool is
expected to serve as a lightweight, open-source, and adaptable platform for thermodynamic learning and research,
particularly for studies involving the Rankine cycle, steam power plants (PLTU), and geothermal energy systems.
Keywords: Steam Table, IAPWS-IF97, CoolProp, Mollier Diagram, Thermodynamic Simulation, Python Application.

1. Introduction
The continuous progress in energy conversion systems during the Industry 4.0 era requires
a thermodynamic analysis that is fast, accurate, and flexible. One of the essential requirements
in this field is determining the thermodynamic properties of working fluids such as water and
steam, which play a crucial role in various energy conversion systems, including steam power
plants (PLTU), geothermal power plants (PLTP), refrigeration cycles, and industrial cooling systems
(Bell, Quoilin, and Wronski, n.d.), (Çengel and Boles 2014). In engineering practice, these property
data are typically obtained from a steam table, which is an empirical compilation derived from
mathematical formulations based on experimental data and thermodynamic equilibrium theory.
This research continues the previous study, "Development of Excel VBA-Based Simulation
Software for Steam Power Plant (PLTU) for Engineering Applications," which focused on
developing a low-cost, educational simulation tool using Microsoft Excel and VBA. Building upon
that foundation, the present work extends the simulation environment into a Python-based
platform with enhanced computational performance, graphical interface, and modular design for
broader engineering and research applications (Manggala, Yerizam, and Effendy 2025).
The international standard for modern water and steam thermodynamic properties is
established by the International Association for the Properties of Water and Steam (IAPWS)
through the IAPWS Industrial Formulation 1997 (IAPWS-IF97) (Wagner and Pruß 2002). This
formulation replaced the previous version (IF-88) by improving both computational accuracy and
speed across a pressure range of 0.611 kPa to 100 MPa and temperatures up to 1073 K (Wagner
et al. 2000). The IAPWS-IF97 formulation defines five distinct regions based on phase conditions
and provides functions for calculating properties such as enthalpy, entropy, specific volume,
internal energy, specific heat, and speed of sound (Figure 1). Direct application of this formulation
yields results with a relative error of less than ±0.1% compared with experimental data.
Consequently, this standard has become the primary reference for power generation and thermal
energy systems industries worldwide.

Figure 1. Region diagram IAPWS-IF97 (P–T log scale)
However, implementing the IAPWS-IF97 formulation in commercial software such as
REFPROP, EES, or MATLAB Cool Toolbox requires paid licenses and specific operating systems.
Furthermore, several of these software packages are closed-source, making them unsuitable for
open research and academic development in higher education institutions, particularly in
developing countries such as Indonesia or Sudan (El-Awad and Elseory 2013). In the context of
energy engineering education, students typically learn thermodynamic calculations using printed
tables or simple spreadsheet tools. This method is inefficient because it requires manual

interpolation and does not support the visualization of graphical relationships among parameters
such as T–s and h–s diagrams. Several universities in Indonesia have developed auxiliary tools
based on Microsoft Excel or MATLAB GUIs, yet these applications are limited to specific platforms
and require licensed software. Therefore, the development of open-access educational software
that is portable, lightweight, and executable without additional installation is a strategic solution.
This approach aligns with the principle of open-source scientific computing, which emphasizes
algorithmic transparency and the reproducibility of computational research results (Hidayat,
Hidayat, and Irawan 2021).
The Python programming language has become a popular choice for scientific software
development due to its extensive community and comprehensive numerical libraries such as
NumPy, SciPy, and Matplotlib. For thermodynamic property modeling, two widely used opensource libraries are IAPWS (Python Implementation) and CoolProp. The iapws library provides a
direct implementation of the IAPWS-IF97 formulation and is particularly suitable for educational
purposes because of its simplicity and transparent source code (Martin, Ranalli, and Moore
2022). Meanwhile, CoolProp, developed by Ian Bell and collaborators, is a reference-quality
thermophysical property library capable of computing over 100 fluids, including water,
refrigerants, and hydrocarbons (Bell, Quoilin, and Wronski, n.d.). It is based on the Helmholtz
energy equation of state and is compatible with multiple programming environments, including
C++, Python, MATLAB, and Excel.
Previous studies have used CoolProp to analyze power cycles, optimize Organic Rankine
Cycles (ORCs), and design heat exchangers. (Banjarnahor, n.d.). However, most of these
implementations still rely on Python environments that require additional dependencies, which
limit their usability in non-programming settings. Although Python offers high-quality
thermodynamic libraries, there has been limited effort to package them into a standalone, userfriendly application featuring an interactive graphical interface that runs without external
installation (Dos Santos Junior, Daltro De Freitas, and Mariano 2025; Iacovino et al. 2021; Otis and
Liu 2017).
This research, therefore, aims to develop a portable Steam Table application in Python
that integrates the IAPWS-IF97 and CoolProp thermodynamic libraries into a single executable
package, featuring a Tkinter-based graphical user interface. The application can compute the
thermodynamic properties of water and steam in real time for P–T, T–s, and h–s parameters,
while providing dynamic visualization through Mollier (h–s) and T–s diagrams. The main novelty
of this study lies in:
1. The simultaneous integration of dual thermodynamic backends (IAPWS & CoolProp) in a
single standalone executable that requires no Python installation or external
dependencies.
2. The implementation of an automated build–release system using batch scripting, which
allows efficient compilation, version control, and consistent reproducibility.
3. The development of an educational interface for validating Rankine cycle processes and
geothermal power systems.
4. The adoption of a reproducible software engineering approach for the creation of open
scientific software in energy engineering education in Indonesia.
2. Research Methodology
This research adopts an engineering software development methodology that combines
computational thermodynamics and reproducible programming principles. The primary stages
include:

1. System design and requirement analysis – defining software scope, backend selection,
and interface layout.
2. Algorithm development implementing property equations using IAPWS-IF97 and
CoolProp libraries.
3. GUI implementation: designing a Python Tkinter interface for interactive use.
4. Build–release automation, developing a batch script pipeline for packaging the project

The research flow can be described as follows: property data for water and steam are
computed using the IAPWS-IF97 and CoolProp backends, processed by Python algorithms, and
displayed in a Tkinter-based GUI with interactive diagrams. Tkinter was chosen as the GUI
framework because it is lightweight, included in the Python standard library, and does not require
additional dependencies. Compared to PyQt or Kivy, which produce larger package sizes and
require external libraries, Tkinter is better suited to the aim of this study: to develop a Steam
Table application that is portable, simple, and easy to distribute as a single executable file to end
users.

2.4. Build Release Automation
To support portability, a batch script automates the entire packaging process:
1. Creating a virtual environment (python -m venv.venv).
2. Installing dependencies (pip install iapws, CoolProp, matplotlib, pyinstaller).
3. Compiling with PyInstaller:
pyinstaller --onefile --windowed ^
--collect-all CoolProp ^
--collect-all iapws ^
steamtable_app\app.py
4. Generating a ZIP release file (SteamTableApp_vYYYYMMDD.zip) and auto-launching the
built EXE.
This ensures version control, consistent builds, and reproducible releases without manual
configuration.

The integration of dual thermodynamic backends, namely IAPWS-IF97 and Cool Prop,
offers distinct advantages for both education and research compared to relying on a single engine.
For educational use, the dual-backend configuration allows students to actively compare property
values obtained from a dedicated industrial standard formulation (IAPWS-IF97) and a more
general multi-fluid library (Cool Prop). This encourages critical thinking about model selection,
numerical consistency, and the influence of property correlations on thermodynamic analyses,
rather than treating steam tables as a "black box". For research and advanced study, the dual
backends provide a built-in cross-checking mechanism that helps identify potential anomalies in
specific regions of the thermodynamic space and facilitates sensitivity studies with respect to the
choice of property source. At the same time, the use of IAPWS-IF97 as a reference ensures
compatibility with established industrial practice in steam power plants and Rankine cycle
analysis.

The introduction of an automated build system based on batch scripting is also a
significant step forward for the local open-source community, particularly in the Indonesian
context. Many students and practitioners work on Windows-based computers with limited
administrative privileges and intermittent internet access, which makes it challenging to install
complex development environments or additional libraries. By encapsulating the compilation
process, dependency handling, and version tagging into a single automated build script, the
project reduces the technical barrier for contributors who wish to modify the source code and
generate their own updated executables. This not only improves reproducibility and traceability
of each release but also encourages collaborative development and reuse of the tool across
different campuses and training centers. In practical terms, the Auto Build feature enables
lecturers and researchers to distribute a consistently built, single-file executable to end users
while maintaining a transparent, open development workflow at the source-code level.

4. Application Limitations
Although the application performs well, several limitations are identified:
1. Single working fluid (water/steam) — only IAPWS-IF97 and CoolProp water models are
integrated; other fluids (e.g., R134a, CO₂) are not yet supported.
2. Process simulation not included — only state-point calculations are implemented;
Rankine or regenerative cycle analysis requires manual post-processing.
3. Operating limits — valid for 273–1073 K and ≤100 MPa; performance outside this range is
unverified.
4. Windows dependency — EXE version tested on Windows only.
5. 2D visualization only — no 3D thermodynamic surface plots.
6. Large binary size — CoolProp increases the EXE file to ~80 MB.
7. GUI-only input — batch or command-line processing not available.
8. No automatic data export — results not yet stored in CSV or database formats.
Thus, the current version serves primarily as an educational and validation tool rather
than a full thermodynamic process simulator.
5. Conclusion
This study successfully developed a standalone Python-based Steam Table application
that integrates IAPWS-IF97 and CoolProp into a single executable package. The program provides
numerically validated, real-time calculations of thermodynamic properties for water–steam
systems, with a maximum deviation of ±0.05% relative to IAPWS-IF97 reference data across all
tested state points. In addition to delivering accurate property values, the application generates

interactive T–s and h–s (Mollier) diagrams, enabling users to directly visualize thermodynamic
processes and better understand the behavior of steam in power generation and related energy
systems. The main achievements of this work can be summarized as follows:
1. Dual-backend integration combining IAPWS-IF97 and CoolProp within one unified
platform, achieving a maximum deviation of less than ±0.05% from reference data while
enabling cross-checking and comparative analysis of property models.
2. An automated build–release pipeline based on batch scripting, which supports one-click
reproducible packaging, consistent versioning, and straightforward distribution of the
application as a single executable file without requiring additional installations or external
dependencies.
3. A lightweight GUI design using Tkinter, which ensures a small package size, stability on
Windows 10–11 environments, and practical usability for both classroom demonstrations
and research-oriented thermodynamic studies.
By combining high numerical accuracy, portability, and ease of deployment, the software
contributes to the development of open-source thermodynamic education tools, particularly in
contexts where access to commercial property libraries is limited. Students and engineers can
perform steam property analysis, construct simple cycle studies, and explore the influence of
different thermodynamic models without needing complex installation procedures or paid
licenses. The dual-backend configuration further encourages critical thinking about model
selection and the impact of property correlations on engineering calculations. Future
developments will focus on extending the application's capabilities beyond the current water–
steam implementation. Planned enhancements include:
