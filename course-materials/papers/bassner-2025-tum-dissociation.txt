Introduction: Generative AI is reshaping programming education, yet its effects on conceptual learning, intrinsic
motivation, and cognitive load remain unclear. This study tests whether assistance deepens understanding or
primarily boosts task completion, and how scaffolded versus answer-giving designs matter.
Objectives: This study compares performance, learning, cognitive load, frustration, and motivation across three
AI support types, and examines students’ perceptions.
Methods: A three-arm randomized controlled trial was conducted in an introductory programming (CS1) course
at TUM (N=275). Participants completed a 90-minute exercise on concurrency, implementing a parallel sum
with threading in one of three conditions: (1) Iris, a scaffolded tutor providing calibrated hints while withholding
full solutions; (2) ChatGPT, unrestricted assistance that can provide complete solutions; (3) no-AI control using
traditional web resources. Pre- and post-knowledge tests and a code comprehension task measured learning, while
auto-graded test coverage measured performance. Validated scales captured intrinsic, germane, and extraneous
cognitive load, frustration, and intrinsic motivation.
Results: Both AI groups achieved substantially higher exercise scores than the control group, with distinct distributions: ChatGPT users clustered at high scores, control participants at low scores, and Iris users spread across the
full range. Despite these performance gains, neither AI condition produced greater pre–post knowledge gains or
code-comprehension advantages. Both AI groups reported lower frustration and reduced extraneous and germane
load than the control group, while intrinsic load did not differ. Only Iris increased intrinsic motivation. Students
rated ChatGPT as easier to use and more helpful.
Conclusion: In this setting, generative AI acted primarily as a performance aid rather than a learning enhancer.
Scaffolded, hint-first design preserved motivational benefits, whereas AI providing unrestricted solutions encouraged a “comfort trap” where students’ preferences misaligned with pedagogical effectiveness. These findings
motivate scaffolded AI integration and assessment designs resilient to environments where performance no longer
reliably tracks understanding.

1. Introduction
Powerful generative artificial intelligence (AI) systems such as
OpenAI’s ChatGPT or GitHub Copilot have upended programming classrooms. Within months of its public release, millions of learners began
pasting homework prompts into large-language-model chat windows
and receiving syntactically flawless code in return. Universities reacted
swiftly—some banning AI outright, others embracing it—yet empirical
evidence of how such tools actually influence learning remains scarce.
The debate is sharply polarized: critics argue that omnipresent code generators will seduce novices into outsourcing cognitive effort, thereby

stunting genuine understanding, whereas proponents highlight AI’s potential to lower frustration, scaffold novices through complex syntax,
and potentially help students access expert-level feedback (Liffiton et al.,
2023; Peng et al., 2023).
Programming education is a particularly revealing domain for studying these tensions because introductory programming courses (CS1)
routinely report high attrition, steep cognitive load, and pronounced
student frustration (Lahtinen et al., 2005). Novices must simultaneously
grapple with abstract problem decomposition, unfamiliar syntax, and incomprehensible compiler errors—challenges that AI might alleviate by

Hidden within this debate lies a more subtle challenge: students
themselves may be poor judges of what helps them learn. Research
consistently shows that learners prefer methods that feel easier, even

et al., 2019). If AI tools make programming feel effortless, will students gravitate toward the very features that undermine their education?

2. Background and related work
Educational chatbots have been deployed across diverse educational contexts (Okonkwo & Ade-Ibijola, 2021), yet their application
in programming education presents a persistent tension: reducing unproductive frustration while preserving productive cognitive effort. This
section examines how AI tutors might navigate this tension. We first
establish theoretical foundations from Cognitive Load Theory, SelfDetermination Theory, and scaffolding research that frame the core
design challenge. We then review empirical evidence on AI in programming education, tracing findings on performance, learning, and affect
before examining scaffolding as a design response. Finally, we synthesize
the methodological gaps this literature reveals and position the study’s
contributions.
2.1. Theoretical foundations: cognition, motivation, and support
Programming education research increasingly draws upon cognitive and motivational frameworks to design and evaluate AI-powered
interventions. Three theoretical perspectives provide essential foundations for understanding how AI tutors influence learning processes,
motivation, and instructional design: Cognitive Load Theory, SelfDetermination Theory, and the concept of scaffolding within the Zone
of Proximal Development. After establishing these foundations, we
examine two cross-cutting challenges—the assistance dilemma and maladaptive help-seeking—that emerge when applying these theories to
instructional design.
Cognitive Load Theory (CLT) distinguishes three types of cognitive load that learners experience during instruction (Paas et al., 2003;
Sweller et al., 1998). Intrinsic load arises from the material’s inherent element interactivity—the extent to which information elements
must be processed simultaneously in working memory. Germane load
represents productive cognitive effort directed toward schema construction and learning. Extraneous load refers to wasted mental effort
imposed by poor instructional design or presentation. Effective instruction seeks to reduce extraneous load, manage intrinsic load within
learners’ working memory capacity, and maximize germane load to promote deep learning. In the context of AI tutors, this framework raises
a critical design tension: while AI support can reduce extraneous load
by providing timely assistance and clarifying misconceptions, it may
also inadvertently undermine learning through multiple mechanisms.

Cognitive offloading—delegating mental work to external tools—creates
a performance-memory trade-off: while offloading improves immediate
task performance, it diminishes subsequent memory for the offloaded
information (Grinschgl et al., 2021). AI tutors providing direct answers may thus enable task completion while bypassing the productive
cognitive struggle essential for durable learning.
Which forms of cognitive effort actually benefit learning? Not
all difficulty is detrimental. Bjork’s framework of desirable difficulties
identifies specific instructional manipulations that temporarily impair
performance during acquisition but enhance long-term retention and
transfer (Bjork et al., 1994). Crucially for AI tutoring, the generation
effect—requiring learners to actively produce answers rather than passively receive them (Slamecka & Graf, 1978)—demonstrates that active
production consistently yields superior retention compared to passive
reception, though the precise cognitive mechanisms remain debated in
the literature. In contrast, difficulties become undesirable when learners
lack the background knowledge or skills to respond to them successfully,
wasting cognitive resources without contributing to schema construction
(Bjork & Bjork, 2011). This distinction maps directly onto CLT’s germane
versus extraneous load dichotomy: effective AI support should eliminate undesirable difficulties while preserving the generative processing
that consolidates learning. Research on affective dynamics suggests
that temporary confusion can signal productive cognitive engagement
when resolved through effort, but prolonged confusion without resolution degrades into frustration and disengagement (D’Mello & Graesser,
2012).
Self-Determination Theory (SDT) offers a complementary motivational perspective, positing that intrinsic motivation—characterized by
interest, enjoyment, and sustained engagement—arises when three basic psychological needs are satisfied: autonomy (experiencing a sense
of volition and choice), competence (feeling effective and capable),
and relatedness (experiencing connection with others) (Ryan & Deci,
2000). Autonomy-supportive learning environments that provide meaningful choices and acknowledge learners’ perspectives foster intrinsic
motivation, whereas controlling environments that pressure learners or
undermine their agency tend to diminish it (Deci & Ryan, 1987). Applied
to AI tutors in programming education, SDT suggests that both hintbased systems and unrestricted answer-providing systems may support
learners’ sense of competence by enabling task completion, yet they
differ substantially in their impact on autonomy: hint-based support preserves learner agency by requiring active problem-solving, while direct
answer provision may undermine autonomy by removing the challenge
and decision-making opportunities that sustain engagement. From this
perspective, excessive assistance may diminish intrinsic motivation even
when performance improves.
The concept of scaffolding, rooted in Vygotsky’s Zone of Proximal
Development (ZPD), provides a third foundational perspective
(Vygotsky, 1978). The ZPD represents the gap between what learners
can accomplish independently and what they can achieve with appropriate support. Scaffolding refers to temporary, calibrated assistance
that enables learners to succeed within their ZPD. Wood et al. (1976)
introduced the scaffolding metaphor to describe tutorial support that
controls task elements initially beyond the learner’s capacity, enabling
them to focus on manageable components. They identified six key
tutoring functions: recruiting learner interest, reducing degrees of
freedom in the task, maintaining direction toward goals, marking
critical features, controlling frustration, and demonstrating solutions
when learners can recognize them. Building on this foundation, subsequent scaffolding research has converged on three key characteristics:
effective support should be contingent (responsive to learner needs),
graduated (adjusting support levels as competence increases), and
eventually faded as learners achieve independence (van de Pol et al.,
2010). This fading requirement connects to CLT: instructional guidance
that benefits novices can become counterproductive for more advanced
learners when external support overlaps with existing knowledge,
forcing learners to expend cognitive resources reconciling redundant

information rather than constructing new schemas (Kalyuga, 2007).
In AI-supported programming education, this framework distinguishes
between systems that provide genuine scaffolding—adaptive hints
that guide without revealing solutions—and those that simply deliver
answers, bypassing the ZPD entirely. The pedagogical challenge lies in
designing AI tutors that maintain learners within their ZPD through
appropriately calibrated support, rather than either abandoning them
without guidance or completing tasks on their behalf.
These three frameworks suggest a potential pedagogical tension:
while AI assistance may reduce extraneous load and help learners work
within their ZPD, it risks undermining learning if it eliminates the
germane cognitive processing essential for schema construction. Two
cross-cutting challenges complicate this picture: the assistance dilemma
and maladaptive help-seeking behaviors.
Providing instructional assistance introduces what Koedinger and
Aleven term the “assistance dilemma” (Koedinger & Aleven, 2007): a
fundamental open problem in instructional science concerning how to
balance information giving and withholding. Too little assistance causes
unproductive frustration and floundering, whereas too much can lead
to shallow processing that bypasses the cognitive engagement necessary
for learning. The optimal level of assistance depends on both learner
characteristics (prior knowledge, expertise level) and task demands
(complexity, cognitive load) (Van Merriënboer & Sweller, 2005). For
instance, a student struggling with thread synchronization might benefit from a hint identifying the need for shared state protection, whereas
receiving a complete synchronized block implementation would eliminate the productive cognitive processing required to understand why
synchronization is necessary and when to apply it. Empirical research on
intelligent tutoring systems demonstrates that moderate support—hints
and guiding questions rather than complete solutions—maximizes learning gains, whereas maximal assistance often proves counterproductive
(Jennings & Muldner, 2021). Generative AI introduces a qualitatively
different context: LLMs can generate complete solutions effortlessly
(Peng et al., 2023), substantially changing the support landscape. The
pedagogical question is therefore not whether AI can provide help, but
how much help is optimal for learning. Whether principles from rulebased intelligent tutoring systems generalize to LLM contexts remains
an open empirical question.
Beyond system design, learner behavior matters: students may not
use help appropriately. Aleven et al. identify a taxonomy of maladaptive help-seeking behaviors: help abuse, whereby students request
hints without genuinely processing their content, and help avoidance,
whereby students persist in unproductive struggle without seeking
assistance when needed (Aleven et al., 2016). Help abuse typically reflects metacognitive difficulties in assessing one’s own understanding,
leading students to request hints preemptively rather than attempting
problems independently (Roll et al., 2011). Help avoidance, in contrast, often stems from performance-avoidant goals—concerns about
demonstrating incompetence relative to others (Karabenick, 2004; Ryan
& Pintrich, 1997). Related to help abuse, Baker et al. demonstrated
that students often “game the system” by systematically exploiting
regularities in tutoring software—requesting help without genuine engagement, clicking through hint sequences without reading them, or
intentionally entering wrong answers to trigger feedback (Baker et al.,
2004).
In sum, the challenge is not whether to provide support, but rather
how to calibrate that support so it preserves the productive cognitive
engagement necessary for genuine expertise rather than performance
that evaporates when support is withdrawn.

2.2.1. Performance and affect
Peng et al. conducted a randomized controlled trial with 95 professional developers recruited through Upwork, tasking participants
with implementing an HTTP server in JavaScript as quickly as possible (Peng et al., 2023). Developers using GitHub Copilot completed
the task 56 % faster, with modestly higher (though not statistically significant) success rates. The study examined a single, standardized task
rather than collaborative professional contexts, measured only completion speed and success without assessing code quality or maintainability,
and explicitly did not evaluate learning outcomes. This exemplifies a
broader gap in AI-assisted programming research: productivity gains are
well-documented, yet learning effects remain largely unexplored, and
professional developers differ substantially from novice learners in both
prior knowledge and learning goals.
Gardella et al. examined 17 novice programmers completing
HumanEval tasks with and without GitHub Copilot under time pressure
(Gardella et al., 2024). Copilot significantly improved performance and
reduced workload as measured by NASA-TLX, yet no significant effects
emerged for emotion or immediate self-efficacy. Drawing on Cognitive
Load Theory, the study revealed that self-efficacy improved only on the
second AI trial, suggesting competence develops through repeated experience rather than immediate tool access. The small sample, controlled
laboratory conditions, brief exposure, and self-efficacy operationalized
through self-grading rather than validated scales constrain generalizability to authentic classroom contexts. Nevertheless, the null affective
findings suggest that reducing workload alone is insufficient without
addressing learners’ sense of capability.
Shihab et al. tasked 10 undergraduate computer science students
with brownfield programming—adding features to unfamiliar legacy
web applications—with and without GitHub Copilot (Shihab et al.,
2025). Students using Copilot achieved substantially faster task completion and more solution progress, while spending less time writing
code and conducting web searches. Yet students expressed concerns
about not understanding how or why Copilot suggestions worked, indicating a comprehension-performance gap. Validity threats include
Copilot’s auto-updates creating inconsistent experimental conditions, a
non-counterbalanced design leaving learning effects unmitigated, and a
small sample of high-achieving students from a single institution.
Fan et al. examined AI-assisted pair programming’s impact on intrinsic motivation among 234 undergraduate students, comparing AI assistance (GPT-3.5 Turbo, Claude 3 Opus), human pairing, and individual
programming (Fan et al., 2025). AI-assisted groups demonstrated significantly increased intrinsic motivation, reduced programming anxiety
compared to individual programming, and significantly outperformed
individual programming (𝑝 < .01), with performance comparable
to human-human pair programming. Grounded in Self-Determination
Theory, the findings attribute motivational gains to enhanced autonomy
(immediate feedback enabling independent problem exploration) and
competence (successful interactions building confidence). Human pairing fostered significantly higher social presence and collaborative depth
than AI assistance, suggesting limitations in fulfilling the relatedness
dimension of SDT. The quasi-experimental design introduces potential
confounding variables, and generalizability may be constrained by the
sample’s institutional and cultural context.
These studies establish that AI assistance accelerates task completion and can enhance motivation, but whether such gains translate to
learning remains unclear.

The most striking empirical evidence for how scaffolding shapes
AI-assisted learning comes from Bastani et al., whose pre-registered randomized controlled trial enrolled nearly 1000 high school mathematics
students across four 90-minute sessions, comparing unrestricted GPT-4,
scaffolded graduated hints, and textbook-only controls (Bastani et al.,
2025). While AI access substantially improved practice performance,
with the scaffolded tutor showing larger gains than unrestricted AI, subsequent examinations administered without AI access revealed a striking
result: unrestricted-AI students performed worse than controls who never
accessed AI, suggesting over-reliance impeded skill acquisition. The scaffolded condition largely mitigated these negative effects, though positive
transfer to independent performance remained elusive. The scaffolded
system provided hints rather than direct answers, requiring students
to interpret guidance and construct their own solutions rather than receiving complete responses immediately. In contrast, unrestricted access
eliminated this regulatory requirement, allowing students to obtain complete solutions without engaging in the diagnostic self-assessment that
signals knowledge gaps. This study provides the strongest causal evidence that scaffolding mitigates AI dependency, though whether these
findings generalize from symbolic mathematics to programming—where
debugging concurrent code requires qualitatively different cognitive
processes—remains untested.
Several LLM-based tutoring systems have operationalized scaffolding
principles for programming education. Huang et al. provide preliminary
evidence from programming education, comparing a scaffolded LLM tutor (CodeFlow Assistant) against traditional teaching assistant support
in a first-year Python course using a quasi-experimental design (Huang
et al., 2025). The CodeFlow Assistant implemented four-tier graduated
scaffolding (flowcharts, cloze coding exercises, basic solutions, and advanced solutions) calibrating support to student needs. The AI group
achieved significantly higher coding performance, yet showed only
comparable improvements in conceptual understanding on assessments
administered without AI—a dissociation between performance and
learning similar to that observed in Bastani et al.’s mathematics study.
Critically, cognitive load analysis revealed that the scaffolded AI significantly enhanced germane load, suggesting greater allocation of working
memory resources to productive learning activities, though effects on intrinsic and extraneous load did not reach significance. The intervention
also significantly improved self-regulated learning abilities, including
intrinsic goal orientation, task value, and metacognitive self-regulation.
The quasi-experimental design with non-random group assignment limits causal inference despite statistical controls, and the comparison to
human TAs rather than unrestricted AI leaves open whether scaffolding
constraints specifically—versus structured assistance generally—drove
the observed benefits.
Liffiton et al. deployed CodeHelp, an LLM-powered assistant with
pedagogical guardrails that provide hints without revealing solutions,
across a 12-week semester with 52 students in an introductory programming course (Liffiton et al., 2023). The system employed a multi-prompt
workflow designed to guide students toward solutions while withholding direct answers. Students reported that CodeHelp was well-received
for its availability and ability to help resolve programming errors.
However, the single-group observational design relied on self-reported
perceptions rather than objective learning outcome measurements, and
the absence of a control condition precludes causal inference about
whether the scaffolding constraints specifically—versus AI assistance
generally—produced observed benefits.

2.3. Research gap and present study

3. The Iris AI-tutor: system design and pedagogical framework

The studies reviewed above converge on several findings while
revealing critical methodological gaps. Performance benefits of AI assistance are well-established: Peng et al. (2023), Gardella et al. (2024),
and Shihab et al. (2025) consistently document faster task completion
and higher success rates with AI tools. Yet these same studies acknowledge that they did not assess learning outcomes, leaving open whether
productivity gains translate to knowledge acquisition.
The scaffolding literature addresses this gap, but with important
limitations. Bastani et al. (2025) mathematics RCT demonstrated that
unrestricted AI access harmed learning relative to no-AI controls, while
scaffolded hints largely mitigated these negative effects. Huang et al.
(2025) provide preliminary evidence from programming, finding that
scaffolded AI yields performance gains but only comparable conceptual
understanding—yet their comparison to human TAs rather than unrestricted AI cannot isolate scaffolding effects. Within programming,
systems like CodeHelp and CodeAid show promise: students report positive experiences and the systems successfully withhold direct solutions.
Yet as Liffiton et al. (2023) and Kazemitabaar et al. (2024) explicitly
note, their single-group deployments without control conditions cannot
establish whether observed benefits stem from scaffolding constraints
specifically or from AI assistance generally.
Affective outcomes add further complexity. Fan et al. (2025) found
that AI assistance increased intrinsic motivation. Gardella et al. (2024)
reported null findings for self-efficacy despite performance gains suggesting that reducing workload alone may be insufficient. These divergent findings likely reflect differences in scaffolding design, yet no
study has directly compared scaffolded and unrestricted AI on affective
outcomes within the same experimental design.
Three specific gaps emerge from this synthesis:
First, no three-arm experimental design in programming education
has isolated scaffolding effects by comparing scaffolded AI, unrestricted
AI, and no-AI conditions simultaneously. Two-arm designs comparing
scaffolded AI against human support (Huang et al., 2025) cannot distinguish scaffolding benefits from AI benefits generally; single-group
deployments of scaffolded systems (Kazemitabaar et al., 2024; Liffiton
et al., 2023) lack the control conditions needed for causal inference.
Second, existing studies fragment outcome measurement across separate investigations. Performance studies (Gardella et al., 2024; Peng
et al., 2023) omit learning assessments; scaffolding studies (Bastani
et al., 2025; Huang et al., 2025) provide limited affective measurements;
affective studies (Fan et al., 2025) rarely include objective learning
outcomes. No single design has measured performance, learning, cognitive load, and motivation comprehensively enough to assess potential
tradeoffs.
Third, how scaffolding constraints shape the interplay between
performance, cognition, and motivation in programming remains unclear. Existing deployments have focused on usability and student
perceptions rather than controlled comparisons of cognitive and motivational outcomes against unrestricted AI. Whether scaffolded AI

The AI tutor Iris, introduced by Bassner et al. (2024), embodies
the scaffolding principles that prior research has identified as critical
for effective educational AI. Unlike external chatbots such as ChatGPT,
Iris leverages deep integration with the Artemis learning-management

traces. This contextual awareness enables Iris to provide situated assistance without requiring students to repeatedly explain their problems

As the primary experimental intervention in this study, Iris operationalizes the design principles emerging from prior work through
three architectural pillars: educational scaffolding via calibrated hints
that preserve productive struggle, a context-aware agent architecture that reasons over LMS data in real time, and a multimodal

3.1. Educational scaffolding: calibrated assistance
Scaffolding theory posits that temporary, tailored support can help
learners bridge knowledge deficits while still fostering independent
problem-solving (Wood et al., 1976). In programming courses, indiscriminately providing full solutions might short-circuit learning and
undermine cognitive engagement. Iris therefore attempts to preserve
productive struggle by reducing unproductive frustration while maintaining the productive cognitive effort needed for mastery (Section 2.1).
Empirical classroom studies confirm that assistants offering partial guidance outperform those providing complete answers (Kazemitabaar et al.,
2024).
Iris modulates help along four ascending tiers of support. At the
most basic level, subtle hints focus attention on salient code lines or
conceptual blind spots without providing explicit solutions. The second
tier employs guiding questions that provoke reflection and self-discovery,
such as asking “How does your loop behave on an empty list?” to encourage students to trace through their logic. Moving up the hierarchy,
high-level conceptual feedback offers strategic guidance without revealing
specific code implementations, helping students understand the broader
approach while preserving the challenge of implementation. Finally, at
the highest level of support, generalized examples illustrate analogous
patterns—such as demonstrating generic recursion principles—while deliberately keeping the target solution opaque to maintain productive
struggle. This four-tier progression constitutes what the authors term
Calibrated Assistance (Bassner et al., 2024), offering two key pedagogical

3.2. A context-aware, dynamic agent
Iris functions as an LLM-powered agent capable of iterative
perception–reasoning–action loops (Xi et al., 2025). Using an ART-style
implementation (Paranjape et al., 2023) for automatic multi-step reasoning and tool use, the agent composes multi-step plans and dynamically
invokes specialized tools (e.g., code-analysis or lecture-retrieval tools)
until it has gathered sufficient evidence to answer. Recent benchmarks
show that such agentic workflows outperform fixed pipelines in openended problem settings (Rawat et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2025). Unlike
external chatbots, Artemis embeds Iris directly within its framework,
granting the tutor secure, automated access to comprehensive contextual information. This integration encompasses problem specifications,
including assignment text and grading rubrics, as well as student artifacts such as current code submissions, prior attempts, and error logs.
Additionally, Iris can access lecture content spanning slide decks and
video transcripts, alongside auxiliary resources including FAQs, documentation, and exemplar solutions. Such holistic context allows Iris to
respond like a human teaching assistant. For instance, upon detecting
repeated test failures, the agent can fetch the failing log, locate the relevant lecture snippet, and generate a hint that addresses the learner’s
conceptual misunderstanding, all without manual copy–paste by the
student. By automatically gathering and integrating relevant artifacts,
Iris reduces the unproductive cognitive demands (Section 2.1) that students would otherwise expend on searching for information, switching
between interfaces, or re-explaining their problem context. This streamlining allows learners to focus their mental resources on understanding
and solving the core programming challenge, rather than on managing
the mechanics of information retrieval.

3.4. Comparison conditions
To contextualize Iris’s design, this study compares it against two
alternatives that differ systematically in the pedagogical dimensions
outlined above.
Unrestricted AI assistance (ChatGPT). General-purpose LLMs like
ChatGPT represent a class of AI tools that provide direct, unconstrained
responses to user queries. Regarding scaffolding, these systems lack pedagogical guardrails: when students request complete solutions, the model
complies. Observational studies of novice programmers using AI code
generation tools report that students frequently accept generated code
with minimal critical evaluation, often struggling to explain or modify solutions they did not construct themselves (Prather et al., 2023).
This stands in contrast to hint-based systems designed to preserve productive struggle. Regarding context-awareness, general-purpose chatbots
operate without access to course materials, exercise specifications, or
student code unless users manually provide this information—unlike
IDE-integrated tools or purpose-built educational assistants. Regarding
grounding, responses draw on the model’s pre-training corpus rather than
authoritative course content; students have noted that ChatGPT “sometimes used functions that were not used in the course” (Kazemitabaar
et al., 2024).
Traditional web resources (No-AI control). Self-directed learning via
web searches, documentation, and Q&A sites like StackOverflow represents the pre-AI baseline in programming education. Unlike AI assistants,
these resources provide no scaffolding in the interactive sense—students
receive static content rather than adaptive guidance calibrated to their
current understanding. There is no context-awareness: generic web content cannot adapt to course-specific requirements or individual student progress. Grounding varies widely, as web content ranges from
authoritative documentation to outdated forum posts, requiring students to independently evaluate source quality. This condition thus
tests whether AI assistance provides genuine pedagogical value beyond
what motivated learners can achieve through traditional self-directed
means.

reducing hallucinations and aligning terminology with course materials
(Li et al., 2025; Shan, 2025).

and provides learners with authoritative follow-up references (Li et al.,

generic chatbots (Horia Alexandru, 2025).

4.3. Programming exercise selection and rationale

concurrency.

SubmissionPublisher. Students needed to initialize thread pools, implement task submission and publishing mechanisms, set up result
consumers, and orchestrate the overall computation flow.

4.6. Experimental procedure
Before the experiment, all tutors participated in a structured preparatory training designed to ensure consistent execution across all tutorial
groups (see Fig. 1). They first received a thorough theoretical introduction to the experiment, covering its objectives, underlying rationale, and

purpose of the study and the sequence of activities. The training then
continued with a complete dry run of the entire procedure, enabling
tutors to experience the experiment from a participant’s perspective

excluded the data collected during this preparatory session. Tutors received a detailed written protocol specifying the sequence of activities,
timing, and instructions to be given to participants. They could consult
an experienced researcher at any time during preparation and execution
to resolve uncertainties or address unforeseen issues. This arrangement
ensured that tutors could conduct the experiment independently within
their sessions while maintaining procedural fidelity across groups.
The announcement stated that students would receive course bonus

finishing the pre- and post-surveys and the programming exercise in a
logical sequence within their assigned tutorial group. The bonus points

Intrinsic Cognitive Load (𝐼𝐶𝐿) represents the mental effort inherent
in the learning material itself and is the answer to the question: How
much information must be understood and connected by the learner
to comprehend the material. Germane Cognitive Load (𝐺𝐶𝐿) reflects
the mental effort devoted to processing and constructing knowledge
schemas. This is the mental effort that leads to learning outcomes
and represents productive cognitive effort. Extraneous Cognitive Load
(𝐸𝐶𝐿) refers to the mental effort caused by poor instructional design
or irrelevant information; thus, it is the load that hinders meaningful
learning and should be minimized.

7. Findings

Both AI-assisted groups successfully reduced student frustration to
similar degrees compared to the NOAI group. However, only IRIS translated this into increased intrinsic motivation, as GPT users showed no
motivational benefits despite their higher exercise performance. This divergence occurred even though both tools enabled similar reductions
in frustration and improvements in exercise completion. Iris’s specific
design features may explain this pattern: its tutor-like interaction style
enables students to overcome problems themselves by scaffolding its
help, while keeping the problem’s solution out of reach. In contrast,
ChatGPT’s approach of providing direct solutions when requested appeared to leave motivation unchanged from baseline levels. The data
suggest that the interaction design and pedagogical approach of an AI
tutor influence motivational outcomes independently of its effects on
performance or frustration.

The analysis reveals four key findings that illuminate how different
AI support modalities influence programming education. These findings
highlight important tensions between performance enhancement and
learning effectiveness. Collectively, they suggest that one interpretation
is that AI tools may function to help students complete challenging programming tasks rather than act as direct learning enhancers, with the
critical distinction lying in how they provide this support.
Finding 1: AI as a Performance, Not Learning, Enhancer
Both AI tools significantly improved exercise performance without enhancing actual learning outcomes. All groups showed no
significant differences in knowledge gains or code comprehension
abilities despite vast differences in task completion rates, suggesting that failing or succeeding in the exercises might not be the
decisive factor for learning.

Finding 4: ChatGPT is a Comfort Trap
Students perceived ChatGPT as significantly more helpful and easier to use, yet these preferences aligned with greater reductions
in learning-related cognitive processing and did not lead to better learning outcomes. This perception-reality gap reveals how
student preferences may mislead pedagogical decisions.

Both AI-assisted groups (IRIS and GPT) achieved substantially higher
exercise performance than the NOAI group, with GPT users reaching
71.84 % compared to 29.85 % for NOAI users, yet this performance advantage did not translate into superior learning. All three groups showed
no significant differences in improvements in conceptual knowledge
and code comprehension abilities. The constant exercise duration across
groups indicates that students didn’t spend more time with AI assistance; they simply accomplished more within the same timeframe.
Both AI groups also reported significantly reduced frustration compared
to the NOAI condition. This stark dissociation between performance
enhancement and learning outcomes suggests that AI tools while improving task performance do not improve learning outcomes, but also
don’t hinder them.

Students consistently rated ChatGPT more favorably than Iris across
multiple measures, including ease of use, feedback quality, query understanding, and perceived learning support. These preferences aligned
with the GPT group’s greater reduction in GCL. Meanwhile, the IRIS
group, which experienced enhanced intrinsic motivation and maintained higher cognitive engagement, received lower ratings on perceived
helpfulness. This inverse relationship between student preferences and
pedagogical outcomes aligns with existing literature showing that students’ perceptions of learning can be inversely related to actual learning
outcomes (Deslauriers et al., 2019), that satisfaction ratings have nearzero correlation with objective learning measures (Uttl et al., 2017), and
that learners often prefer easier but less effective methods (Yan et al.,
2016).

Finding 2: Iris Balances Scaffolding and Cognitive Challenge
Exercise performance distributions revealed distinct patterns: GPT
users clustered at high scores, NOAI users at low scores, while
IRIS users were distributed across the full range. This preservation of performance variation, combined with germane cognitive
load patterns, suggests that Iris achieves a unique balance between
support and challenge.

8. Discussion
This study reveals an intriguing relationship in AI-supported programming education: tools that significantly improve exercise performance do not automatically enhance conceptual learning. These findings
must be interpreted within the constraints of the study’s learning measures. Nevertheless, they indicate an interesting relationship between
student success and understanding, while illuminating critical design
considerations for educational AI systems.
In the following sections, this discussion examines the mechanisms
underlying the performance-learning dissociation, explores how different scaffolding approaches affect student engagement and cognitive
load, analyzes the motivational consequences of AI assistance design, examines the perception-reality gap in student preferences, and discusses
the implications for educational practice and future research.

GPT users clustered at high performance levels, creating a ceiling effect where nearly all students achieved similar high scores. The
NOAI group showed the opposite pattern, clustering at the lower end
of the performance spectrum. IRIS, however, produced a markedly
different distribution: students spread across the entire performance
range, preserving individual differences in achievement. The cognitive
load data revealed a parallel pattern. While both AI tools significantly
reduced GCL compared to the NOAI condition, the GPT group’s reduction was descriptively larger than the IRIS group’s. Though the
direct comparison between AI tools did not reach statistical significance, the combination of performance distributions and cognitive load
patterns suggests different mechanisms of support between the two AI
systems.

8.1. The performance-learning dissociation
The complete dissociation between exercise performance and learning outcomes (Finding 1) raises important questions about AI’s role in
education. One interpretation of this pattern is that AI may function
in programming education by helping students complete constantdifficulty tasks across the knowledge spectrum rather than enhancing learning directly, although the learning measures employed were
limited.
One lens for understanding these results is that traditional programming exercises of constant difficulty create a binary outcome: students
with sufficient background knowledge succeed while others struggle

between a motivating challenge, an easy task, and a frustrating
experience.

8.3. Autonomy and motivation: how scaffolding design shapes engagement
The motivational findings reveal that how AI supports task completion matters as much as whether it provides support (Finding 3). Both
tools reduced frustration without significant differences between them,
yet only Iris enhanced intrinsic motivation. This divergence illuminates
an interesting distinction: ChatGPT enables completion by removing the
challenge (revealing solutions), while Iris supports engagement by scaffolding interaction with the challenge (offering hints without revealing
answers). The difference is between making a task easy versus making
it approachable.
Self-Determination Theory (SDT) provides a theoretical lens for
understanding this pattern (Ryan & Deci, 2000). According to this framework, intrinsic motivation depends on satisfying three basic psychological needs: autonomy (experiencing volition and choice), competence
(feeling effective), and relatedness (connection with others). While both
AI tools may have supported feelings of competence by enabling task
completion, they differ in their impact on autonomy. Iris’s hint-based
design preserved learner autonomy by withholding complete solutions
and requiring students to construct their own understanding, maintaining what De Charms (1983) called an “internal locus of causality”—the
sense that one’s actions stem from personal volition rather than external
control. ChatGPT’s direct-answer approach, by contrast, eliminated autonomous problem-solving and produced no motivational benefit over
traditional resources. This pattern aligns with SDT’s prediction that controlling environments undermine intrinsic motivation even when they
are helpful (Deci & Ryan, 1987).
This suggests that effective support in education may not be about
removing all barriers but about selectively helping students overcome
unnecessary obstacles while preserving those that promote growth.
ChatGPT makes everyone succeed uniformly, while Iris makes everyone able to try meaningfully. This distinction is shaped by pedagogical
design rather than mere technological capability.
However, the motivational advantage did not translate into a learning advantage within this study. SDT research has found that autonomysupportive conditions produce greater conceptual learning compared to
controlling conditions, even when rote recall does not differ (Grolnick
& Ryan, 1987). Ryan and Deci summarize this pattern as students
in controlling environments learning “less effectively, especially when
learning requires conceptual, creative processing” (Ryan & Deci, 2000).
Yet no group differences emerged on the Knowledge Assessment or
Code Comprehension measures, both of which assessed conceptual
understanding rather than rote recall.
Several boundary conditions may explain this null finding. First, the
90-minute session may have been too brief for differential motivation to
influence knowledge consolidation—Grolnick and Ryan’s study found
effects on retention more pronounced at an 8-day follow-up. Second,
concurrent programming’s procedural implementation demands may
differ from the text comprehension tasks where SDT’s learning effects
have been demonstrated. Third, the learning measures may not have
captured subtle differences in understanding that our instruments were
not designed to detect.

8.2. Scaffolding and the preservation of productive challenge
The distribution patterns of exercise performance reveal differences
in how AI tools support student performance on challenging tasks
(Finding 2). ChatGPT appears to have “lifted” nearly all students to
high performance levels, essentially removing the challenge entirely and
enabling uniform completion of difficult tasks. In contrast, the NOAI
group showed limited success, with the constant-difficulty task proving
challenging for most regardless of effort. Iris’s pattern suggests something different: calibrated assistance. This approach offers enough help
for students to approach tasks without revealing solutions. It preserves
individual differences while potentially enabling broader engagement.
This preservation of performance variation suggests that Iris’s scaffolded approach maintains what Vygotsky termed the “zone of proximal
development,” the space where learning is both challenging and achievable (Vygotsky, 1978). The calibrated approach allows students to
receive sufficient support to engage with constant-difficulty material
without eliminating the productive struggle that drives understanding.
The cognitive load data provide a mechanistic explanation for these
patterns. While both AI tools reduced GCL and ECL compared to the
NOAI group, ChatGPT’s reduction was descriptively more pronounced.
Following Sweller’s cognitive load framework (Sweller et al., 1998), germane load represents the mental effort devoted to schema construction
and automation, the very processes that drive learning. Lower germane
processing means that students did not invest as much active cognitive
effort (Klepsch et al., 2017; Klepsch & Seufert, 2021), which suggests
either improved efficiency (support reduces the amount of learningoriented processing needed) or dampened investment (support reduces
engagement in learning-relevant processing).
This finding aligns with prior research on intelligent tutoring systems
showing that moderate scaffolding outperforms both minimal and maximal support (Jennings & Muldner, 2021). Iris appears to have found this
“sweet spot,” providing enough assistance to prevent frustration while
preserving sufficient challenge to maintain cognitive engagement. The
tool’s design, offering hints and guidance without revealing solutions,
exemplifies the scaffolding functions Wood et al. (1976) identified: it
recruits student engagement, reduces task complexity to manageable
levels, maintains direction toward learning goals, marks critical features
requiring attention, controls frustration, and demonstrates concepts
without eliminating the challenge.
The simultaneous reduction of GCL and ECL presents a theoretical
tension. When both extraneous and germane load are reduced simultaneously, Cognitive Load Theory predicts diminished learning because
less capacity is engaged in schema-building processes, even if the task
feels easier. Both AI conditions reduced extraneous and germane load
compared to the control group, yet all three groups showed equivalent
knowledge gains from T1 to T2—not the worse learning outcomes that
CLT would predict.

8.5. Practical implications for educators and institutions
This study derives four actionable insights for educational practice.
First, educators should carefully consider their goals when integrating
AI tools. If the objective is to reduce frustration and improve course
completion rates, both ChatGPT and Iris appear effective. However,
if the goal is to enhance intrinsic motivation, only Iris demonstrated
benefits—suggesting that scaffolded approaches better support sustained engagement. If the goal is to enhance conceptual understanding,
neither tool showed advantages over traditional resources, at least in the
short term as measured by this study.
Second, the findings suggest that AI tools might be most valuable
for helping students overcome critical hurdles rather than as learning enhancers. For students who might otherwise struggle to complete
assignments due to syntactic difficulties or debugging challenges, AI assistance could mean the difference between engagement and dropout.
Notably, if the goal is to help students past the hardest obstacles
while preserving productive challenge, scaffolded tools like Iris may
be preferable to unrestricted AI, which tends to smooth over difficulties uniformly. This interpretation may help educators set appropriate
expectations and design complementary learning activities.
Third, assessment practices and course design must evolve to maintain validity and align incentives with learning goals. In practice and
formative homework, instructors can default to hint-first assistance with
gated escalation, and award credit for process evidence, such as tests,
diffs, and brief rationales, so the pedagogically desirable path also
becomes the easiest route to points. In summative contexts, the focus
should shift to code reading, debugging, explanation, and delayed or
transfer items that resist superficial solution delivery. These choices
align incentives with learning goals rather than with raw completion,
addressing the assessment validity concerns raised by the performancelearning dissociation.
Finally, for institutions navigating AI policy, the findings suggest that
scaffolded AI systems represent a defensible middle ground between outright AI bans and unrestricted access. Scaffolded tools like Iris preserve
motivational benefits while avoiding the perception trap that makes unrestricted AI pedagogically concerning. The policy question shifts from
“whether to permit AI” to “how to design AI that supports learning
goals.”

8.4. The perception-reality gap: the comfort trap of effortless tools
The systematic preference for ChatGPT across multiple dimensions
reveals a troubling disconnect between student perceptions and pedagogical effectiveness (Finding 4). Students appear to gravitate toward
tools that make tasks feel easier, yet this preference for comfort is pedagogically counterproductive. The tool students perceived as most helpful
was also the one that most reduced their GCL, the very mental effort
associated with building understanding. This interpretation is likewise
constrained by the limited reliability of the two-item GCL subscales.
Meanwhile, Iris received lower ratings on perceived helpfulness. This
occurred despite Iris maintaining descriptively better cognitive engagement and significantly enhancing motivation.
This “comfort trap” reveals a fundamental challenge in educational
technology: students cannot reliably judge what best supports their
learning based on subjective experience alone. What feels most helpful, maximum support with minimal effort, may actually undermine the
productive struggle necessary for deep learning. This finding cautions
against using student satisfaction as the primary metric for evaluating
educational AI tools and aligns with extensive research showing that
learners are poor judges of their own learning. Deslauriers et al. (2019)
demonstrated that students consistently rate fluent, passive instruction more favorably than active learning approaches, despite learning
significantly less.
The preference for ChatGPT appears to stem from its ability to
minimize cognitive effort, precisely the characteristic that may limit
its pedagogical effectiveness. This creates tension for educators and
policymakers: should student satisfaction or learning effectiveness be
prioritized? The marketing pressures and student-as-customer mentality
prevalent in higher education may push institutions toward adopting
tools that students prefer, even when those tools may be pedagogically
suboptimal.
This pattern extends research on metacognitive illusions to the domain of AI tool selection. When a tool makes tasks feel effortless,
students may interpret this fluency as evidence of effective learning
support, even when underlying processes that sustain engagement have
been bypassed. The mechanism may run deeper: metacognitive monitoring itself might constitute a form of germane cognitive load (Klepsch
& Seufert, 2020). If so, the reduced germane load observed in AI conditions may have directly impaired students’ ability to accurately judge
their own learning—the very tool that feels most helpful may undermine
the cognitive processes needed to recognize that feeling as misleading.

about the learning process itself. If students understood that productive struggle enhances retention, they might make different choices

8.6. Future directions and open questions
This study opens several avenues for future research. Longitudinal
investigations could examine whether the performance-learning dissociation persists over time or whether early AI assistance influences later
unassisted performance. Studies examining transfer tasks, problems requiring application of learned concepts in novel contexts, could provide
deeper insights into the quality of AI-supported learning.
The theoretical findings raise questions that warrant further investigation. The fractionation of the assistance dilemma into separable
affective and cognitive dimensions—where Iris resolved the dilemma for
motivation but not for learning—may or may not generalize to other educational domains and longer timeframes. Similarly, Self-Determination
Theory predicted that autonomy-supportive conditions would enhance
conceptual learning, yet no learning differences emerged; future work
should examine whether the 90-minute timeframe was too brief for such
effects to manifest, or whether programming tasks differ fundamentally
from the text comprehension domains where SDT’s learning effects have
been established. The equivalent learning outcomes despite reduced germane cognitive load also challenge Cognitive Load Theory predictions
and merit further theoretical attention.
The optimal design of scaffolded AI systems remains underspecified. While Iris’s approach showed motivational advantages over unrestricted AI, the critical design features remain unclear. Future research

9. Threats to validity

Reliability addresses whether the study can be replicated with consistent results. The absence of delayed testing prevents conclusions about
retention or the durability of any observed learning effects. Additionally,
while this paper provides detailed descriptions of the experimental protocol and materials to support replication, the rapidly evolving nature
of AI tools means that exact replication with identical tool capabilities
may become impossible as these systems continue to develop. However,
the exercise, surveys, and LMS configuration were fixed across all 35
sessions; we document the full tutor script and procedure; and we release the used exercise, survey questions, pseudonymized dataset and
analysis code, enabling computational reproducibility and facilitating
conceptual replications even as specific model versions change. While
ICL and GCL subscales contain only two items each, which limits traditional reliability assessment, the PCA analysis confirms that all items
contribute meaningfully to the overall cognitive load construct. Results
for two-item subscales should be interpreted with caution.

9.4. Reliability

Following Runeson and Höst’s validity framework for empirical
software engineering research (Runeson & Höst, 2009), this analysis
identifies several threats to the validity of these findings that must be
considered when interpreting results.
9.1. Construct validity
Construct validity concerns whether we measured what we intended to measure. A few threats arise from the assessment design
employed. The knowledge assessment relied on six conceptual questions
and a three-item code comprehension task, which may inadequately
capture the multifaceted nature of programming understanding; the
coarse granularity of the Code Comprehension measure (0–3 points)
may reduce sensitivity to subtle effects. The study included no additional programming tasks or code-writing assessments that would test
students’ ability to apply learned concepts in novel contexts without
AI support. These assessment limitations mean that null findings for
learning outcomes should be interpreted cautiously. However, the combination of two learning measures (conceptual knowledge and code
comprehension), objective LMS-based performance data, and validated
psychological scales with acceptable-to-high internal consistency (reported in Section 4.4) provides converging evidence; the pre- and post
identical-item design increases sensitivity to within-student change; and
all instruments were tightly aligned with the concurrency content.

10. Conclusion
The pattern of reduced stress without detectable learning differences
observed in this study reveals strong tensions in AI-supported programming education. The findings demonstrate that generative AI tools
can improve exercise performance—with the ChatGPT Group achieving
scores 2.4 times higher than the No-AI control group—while showing no
significant differences in conceptual understanding. This performancelearning dissociation challenges the field to reconsider what “success”
means in an era when AI can eliminate traditional barriers to task
completion.
Perhaps more troubling is the comfort trap uncovered by this study:
students systematically preferred ChatGPT’s direct solutions over Iris’s
scaffolded guidance, rating it as more helpful and easier to use, despite
ChatGPT offering no learning or motivational advantages. This preference for the pedagogically weaker tool exposes a critical vulnerability in
student-driven adoption of educational technology. When learners cannot distinguish between what feels helpful and what actually promotes
understanding, market forces may push institutions toward tools that
maximize satisfaction at the expense of learning.
Yet the results also point toward a more nuanced path forward.
Iris demonstrated that thoughtfully designed AI support can thread the
needle between assistance and challenge—reducing frustration while
uniquely enhancing intrinsic motivation. The key lies not in AI’s raw
capabilities but in its pedagogical implementation: scaffolded hints
that preserve student agency are more educationally valuable than unrestricted solution access, even when students perceive them as less
helpful. This finding underscores that the question is not whether to
use AI in education, but how to design AI systems that support genuine
learning rather than merely enabling task completion.
As programming education stands at this crossroads, this study offers both warnings and guideposts. The warnings are clear: AI tools that
maximize performance and minimize effort may create an illusion of
learning that satisfies students while showing no statistical improvement

9.2. Internal validity
Internal validity addresses whether observed relationships are causal
rather than spurious. The high attrition rate (39.2 % of enrolled participants) may have introduced systematic selection bias, as students
who completed the study following all the rules may differ meaningfully from those who withdrew or were excluded due to attention
check failures or timing issues. The experimental context itself may
have influenced behavior through Hawthorne effects, where participants modify their behavior due to awareness of being observed.
However, random assignment yielded equivalent groups in terms of
gender, age, and prior programming experience (Table 3); exclusion
rules were pre-specified and applied uniformly across arms; attrition
proportions were comparable between conditions; sessions followed a
standardized, tutor-training-backed script with in-room supervision; and
multiple compliance checks such as attention items and temporal-order
constraints collectively mitigate validity issues.
9.3. External validity
External validity concerns the generalizability of findings beyond the
studied context. While this study addresses methodological gaps in prior
work through its three-arm design and controlled model comparison,
generalizability remains constrained by several contextual factors: data
collection at a single institution, within one introductory programming
